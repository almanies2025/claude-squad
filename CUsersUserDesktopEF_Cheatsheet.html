1   VC METHOD — Post-Money vs Pre-Money Valuation
Post-Money Val = Pre-Money Val + Investment  |  Founder Shares = (1 − Ownership%) × Post-Money Val
  • Step 1: Identify pre-money valuation (what investors think company is worth before this round)
  • Step 2: Add investment amount → gives post-money valuation
  • Step 3: Founder ownership after round = Investment ÷ Post-Money Val (for simple 1-round case)
  • Step 4: Founder % = (1 − Investor%) × Post-Money Val — OR — find shares directly
Worked Example — VC Method
Given: Pre-money = $4M  |  Investment = $1M  |  Post-money = $5M
Q1: What is founder ownership %?
  Post-Money Val = $4M + $1M = $5M
  Founder % = $4M ÷ $5M = 80%
  Investor % = $1M ÷ $5M = 20%

Q2: Founders have 8M shares. What is price per share (PPS)?
  Total Shares = 8M ÷ 80% = 10M shares
  PPS = $5M ÷ 10M = $0.50 per share

Q3: New investors put in $2M at $2 PPS. Pre-money = ?
  New shares issued = $2M ÷ $2 = 1M shares
  Pre-money = 9M existing shares × $2 = $18M
2   DILUTION — Option Pool & New Round
Dilution % = New Shares ÷ (Existing Shares + New Shares)  |  Post-Dilution % = Pre-Dilution % × (1 − Dilution%)
  • Option Pool (ESOP): Created BEFORE new investment — comes from founder shares
  • Dilution formula: New investor shares ÷ (existing + new) = investor %
  • Post-dilution founder %: Take your old %, multiply by (1 − total dilution %)
Worked Example — Dilution
Given: Pre-round: Founders 8M shs (80%) | Option pool 2M shs (20%) | Total 10M

Round 2: New investor puts $3M at $1.50/share → 2M new shares
  New total shares = 10M + 2M = 12M
  Investor % = 2M ÷ 12M = 16.67%
  Founder dilution = 2M ÷ 12M = 16.67% (same as investor, taken pro-rata)
  Founder new % = 80% × (1 − 0.1667) = 66.67%
  Option pool dilution = 20% × (1 − 0.1667) = 16.67%
  Check: 66.67% + 16.67% + 16.67% = 100% ✓

Key insight: Option pool and founder shares both dilute when new money enters — the pool does NOT protect founders
3   SAFE NOTES — Valuation Cap vs Discount
SAFE Cap: Price = Min(Cap ÷ Shares, Discount × Price)  |  SAFE Proceeds = Shares × Series Price
  • SAFE = Simple Agreement for Future Equity — not a loan, no interest, no maturity
  • Valuation Cap: Investor gets the LOWER of (cap price) OR (discounted Series A price)
  • Discount: e.g., 20% discount → investor pays 80% of Series A price
  • Post-Money SAFE: Ownership % is fixed at issuance (most common now)
Worked Example — SAFE Valuation Cap
Given: SAFE cap = $5M | Discount = 20% | Series A pre-money = $8M | 10M shares pre-SAFE

Method 1 (Cap):
  Cap price = $5M ÷ 10M = $0.50/share

Method 2 (Discount):
  Series A price = $8M ÷ 10M = $0.80/share
  Discounted price = $0.80 × 0.80 = $0.64/share

SAFE investor gets: $0.50/share (cap wins, it's lower)
  vs Series A investor who pays $0.80/share → SAFE investor gets 20% more shares for same $
Professor tip: SAFE cap always beats discount when cap price < discounted Series A price. Calculate both!
4   CONVERTIBLE NOTES — Interest + Cap + Discount
Accrued Value = Principal × (1 + Rate%)^years  |  Conversion Price = Min(Cap, Discount × Series Price)
  • Unlike SAFE: Convertible notes HAVE interest (rate%) AND maturity date AND are debt instruments
  • Interest accrues: Principal × Rate% × Years — added to principal before conversion
  • Cap vs Discount: Same logic as SAFE — investor takes the better of both
  • Maturity: If not converted, note must be repaid (unlike SAFE which just converts)
Worked Example — Convertible Note
Given: $500K note | 8% p.a. interest | 2-year term | Cap = $4M | Discount = 25%
                Series A pre-money = $6M | 6M shares pre-note

  Accrued principal = $500K × (1.08)² = $583.2K
  Cap price = $4M ÷ 6M = $0.667/share
  Discounted Series A price = ($6M ÷ 6M) × 0.75 = $0.75/share
  Conversion price = Min($0.667, $0.75) = $0.667/share
  Note converts to shares = $583.2K ÷ $0.667 = 874K shares
  Note holder ownership% = 874K ÷ (6M + 874K) = 12.7%

Critical: "Pre-money valuation" is the pre-round total value. Always divide investment by post-money to get investor %.
6   LIQUIDATION PREFERENCE — 1x vs 2x Participating
1x Non-Participating: Min(1×Investment, Proceeds) + Pro-Rata  |  Participating: Investment + (Remaining × Ownership%)
  • Liquidation preference: How much investors get paid FIRST before founders in an exit
  • 1× non-participating: Investor gets 1× their money OR their pro-rata share — whichever is MORE
  • 2× non-participating: Investor gets 2× investment before anyone else
  • Participating: Investor gets their preference AND shares pro-rata in what's left
Worked Example — Liquidation Preference
Given: Exit = $15M | Investor put in $5M | Investor owns 30% | Preference = 1× non-participating

  Preference amount = 1 × $5M = $5M
  Pro-rata share = 30% × $15M = $4.5M
  Investor chooses = Max($5M, $4.5M) = $5M (preference wins)
  Remaining for all = $15M − $5M = $10M
  Founder gets = 70% × $10M = $7M | Investor = $5M fixed ✓

Now 2× non-participating:
  Preference = 2 × $5M = $10M
  Remaining = $15M − $10M = $5M → Founders get $5M | Investor = $10M

Now 1× PARTICIPATING:
  Investor takes 1× = $5M first, then 30% of remaining $10M = $3M
  Total investor = $5M + $3M = $8M | Founders = $7M
  → Participating investors do VERY well when exit > 1× investment
7   TERM SHEET CLAUSES — What Each Means
Anti-Dilution (Ratchet)Broad-based: uses all shares (incl. options). Full-ratchet: replaces founder shares at new low price. Protects investors, hurts founders.
Liquidation Pref (1×)Investor gets 1× investment first. Non-participating = they choose pref OR pro-rata (whichever is better). Participating = both.
Pay-to-PlayInvestors who don't participate in future rounds lose anti-dilution protection or board seats. Keeps cap table clean.
Pro-Rata RightsInvestors can maintain their % by buying in future rounds. Founders hate this — dilutes their ability to lead rounds.
Board CompositionCommon: 2 founders + 1 investor + 1 independent. Investors want more seats for control. Watch for uneven splits.
Vesting (Acceleration)Single-trigger: vests on acquisition. Double-trigger: vests on acquisition + being fired. Founders prefer double-trigger.
Dividends (Cumulative)Accrues if unpaid. Adds to liquidation preference at exit. Cumulative vs non-cumulative matters a LOT in long holds.
Exam question type: "What are the implications of [clause] for founders vs investors?" — always compare BOTH sides.
8   ARR, CHURN & NET NEW ARR
ARR = MRR × 12  |  Churn Rate = Lost Customers ÷ Start Customers  |  Net New ARR = New ARR + Expansion − Churned ARR
  • ARR: Annual Recurring Revenue — annualized subscription revenue. MRR × 12.
  • Churn Rate: % of customers lost in a period. Higher churn = need more new customers just to maintain revenue.
  • Net New ARR: Best metric. Shows true growth after accounting for losses.
Worked Example — ARR & Churn
Given: Jan MRR = $50K | Feb: +$8K new, −$3K churn | Mar: +$12K new, −$2K churn

  Jan ARR = $50K × 12 = $600K
  Feb Net New = +$8K − $3K = +$5K MRR
  Feb ARR = $55K × 12 = $660K
  Feb Churn Rate = $3K ÷ $50K = 6% per month

  Mar Net New = +$12K − $2K = +$10K MRR
  Mar ARR = $65K × 12 = $780K
  3-month Net New ARR = $780K − $600K = $180K

Rule of 40: ARR growth% + profit margin% ≥ 40 is healthy SaaS.
  If growing 80% ARR with −20% margin = 60 → PASSES Rule of 40 ✓

9   CAC, LTV & CAC/LTV RATIO
CAC = Total Sales & Marketing Cost ÷ New Customers  |  LTV = ARPU × Gross Margin % ÷ Churn Rate  |  LTV/CAC ≥ 3
  • CAC: Total cost to acquire ONE new customer (all sales + marketing spend ÷ new customers)
  • LTV: Total gross profit expected from one customer over their lifetime
  • LTV/CAC ≥ 3: Industry rule of thumb. Below 3 = burning money. Above 5 = under-investing in growth.
  • ARPU: Average Revenue Per User per month
Worked Example — CAC & LTV
Given: Sales team = $120K/yr | Marketing = $80K/yr | 100 new customers/yr | ARPU = $500/mo | Gross margin = 70% | Monthly churn = 3%

  CAC = ($120K + $80K) ÷ 100 = $2,000 per customer

  Monthly revenue per user = $500
  Gross profit per user/mo = $500 × 70% = $350
  Avg customer lifetime = 1 ÷ 3% = 33.3 months
  LTV = $350 × 33.3 = $11,667 per customer

  LTV/CAC Ratio = $11,667 ÷ $2,000 = 5.8×
  → 5.8× > 3 ✓ Excellent — for every $1 spent on acquisition, you get $5.80 back
Watch out: LTV formula varies. Some use ARPU × Gross Margin × (1/Churn Rate). Same answer, different route. Always show your work.
10   IRR — Internal Rate of Return
NPV = 0 at IRR  |  IRR = Discount rate where Σ(Cash Flow_t ÷ (1+IRR)^t) = 0  |  Use interpolation or Excel
  • IRR: The discount rate that makes NPV = 0. Higher IRR = better return for same risk.
  • For VC: IRR is what VC needs to hit their target return (e.g., 25-30% for early-stage)
  • Simple IRR: Total return over hold period = (Exit ÷ Investment)^(1÷Years) − 1 (works for 1-period, no interim cash flows)
Worked Example — IRR (Single Investment)
Given: Invest $2M | Exit at $10M | 5-year hold | No interim cash flows

  Multiple (MOIC) = $10M ÷ $2M = 5.0×
  Simple IRR formula = (5.0)^(1÷5) − 1
  5^(0.2) = 1.3797 − 1 = 37.97% IRR

With interim cash flows — try two discount rates:
Year 0: −$2M | Year 2: +$1M return | Year 5: +$5M
  Try 20%: −2 + 1/(1.2)² + 5/(1.2)^5 = −2 + 0.694 + 2.01 = +0.70 → NPV > 0
  Try 30%: −2 + 1/(1.3)² + 5/(1.3)^5 = −2 + 0.591 + 1.35 = −0.06 → NPV < 0
  IRR is between 20% and 30% ≈ 28-29%

With Excel: =IRR({-2000000, 1000000, 0, 0, 5000000}) = ~28.6%
Quick IRR estimate: For 5× return in 5 years, IRR ≈ 38%. For 3× in 3 years, IRR ≈ 44%. These are close enough for the exam.
11   DCF — Discounted Cash Flow
Enterprise Value = Σ [ FCF_t ÷ (1+WACC)^t ] + TV ÷ (1+WACC)^n  |  TV = FCF_n × (1+g) ÷ (WACC−g)
  • Step 1: Project Free Cash Flows (FCF) for 5-10 years
  • Step 2: Calculate Terminal Value at end of forecast period using Gordon Growth Model
  • Step 3: Discount each FCF and TV back to today using WACC
  • Step 4: Sum all discounted cash flows = Enterprise Value
Worked Example — Simplified DCF
Given: FCF Y1=$100K, Y2=$120K, Y3=$150K | WACC=12% | g=3% | Terminal FCF at Y3 × (1+g)

  Terminal Value at Y3 = $150K×1.03 ÷ (0.12−0.03) = $154.5K ÷ 0.09 = $1,717K

  PV(Y1) = 100 ÷ 1.12 = $89.3K
  PV(Y2) = 120 ÷ 1.12² = 120 ÷ 1.2544 = $95.7K
  PV(Y3) = 150 ÷ 1.12³ = 150 ÷ 1.4049 = $106.8K
  PV(TV) = 1,717 ÷ 1.12³ = 1,717 ÷ 1.4049 = $1,222.2K

  Enterprise Value = 89.3 + 95.7 + 106.8 + 1,222.2 = $1,514K ≈ $1.51M
12   WACC — Weighted Average Cost of Capital
WACC = (E÷V × Re) + (D÷V × Rd × (1−T))  |  E=Equity, D=Debt, V=E+D, Re=Cost of equity, Rd=Cost of debt, T=Tax rate
  • E/V: Equity as % of total capital (market cap weight, not book)
  • D/V: Debt as % of total capital
  • Re (Cost of equity): Use CAPM = Rf + β(Rm−Rf) or given rate
  • After-tax cost of debt: Rd × (1 − T) — interest is tax-deductible
Worked Example — WACC
Given: Equity = $8M | Debt = $2M | Re = 18% | Rd = 8% | Tax rate = 25%

  Total Capital V = $8M + $2M = $10M
  E/V = $8M ÷ $10M = 80%
  D/V = $2M ÷ $10M = 20%
  After-tax Rd = 8% × (1 − 0.25) = 6%

  WACC = (0.80 × 18%) + (0.20 × 6%)
          = 14.4% + 1.2% = 15.6%

Note: WACC is used as the discount rate in DCF. Higher WACC → lower enterprise value (money today is worth less when you discount heavily).
13   EPS & TREASURY STOCK METHOD
EPS = (Net Income − Preferred Dividends) ÷ Weighted Avg Shares  |  Diluted Shares = Basic + In-the-money Options + Warrants
  • Diluted EPS: Uses all potentially dilutive securities (options, warrants, convertible debt)
  • Treasury Stock Method (TSM): Calculate dilutive effect of in-the-money options:
  • TSM Step 1: Options in the money = exercise price < current share price
  • TSM Step 2: Shares issued − shares repurchased at current price = net dilution
Worked Example — Treasury Stock Method
Given: Net Income = $1M | Shares outstanding = 500K | Tax rate = 30%
           Options: 100K at $10 exercise | Current share price = $25

  Basic EPS = $1M ÷ 500K = $2.00 per share

  Proceeds from exercise = 100K × $10 = $1M
  Shares repurchased (at $25) = $1M ÷ $25 = 40K shares
  Net new shares (dilution) = 100K − 40K = 60K shares
  Diluted shares = 500K + 60K = 560K
  Diluted EPS = $1M ÷ 560K = $1.79 per share

  Note: Diluted EPS ($1.79) < Basic EPS ($2.00) — dilution is real, not hypothetical
TSM caveat: Only count options where exercise price < current price. Out-of-the-money options have ZERO dilutive effect.
14   BOOTSTRAPPING vs ANGEL vs VC — Comparison
BootstrappingSelf-funded. Revenue-first. Slow growth but full control. No dilution. Best for: SaaS, services, lifestyle businesses.
Angel Investor$25K–$250K typically. Personal money. Mentorship. Usually takes 10-20% equity. Good for pre-revenue.
VC (Venture Capital)$500K–$10M+. Professional investors. Board seat. Heavy dilution. Good for: high-growth, high-burn startups.
CrowdfundingEquity or reward-based. Validates market demand. Many small investors. Regulatory limits apply.
SAFE vs Convertible NoteSAFE = no interest, no maturity, no debt. Note = interest accrues, has maturity date, is debt on books. SAFE is simpler.
When to RaiseRaise when: (1) you have traction to negotiate, (2) you have a specific use of capital, (3) the opportunity requires speed you can't bootstrap.
Berkus Method: Pre-revenue valuation = sum of 5 criteria × $200-500K each. Criteria: Sound idea, prototype, team, relationships, sales!
15   EXIT MATH — M&A vs IPO vs Secondary
M&A IRR = (Exit Proceeds ÷ Investment)^(1÷Years) − 1  |  IPO Lock-up = 180 days typically  |  Secondary = selling existing shares pre-exit
Worked Example — Exit Scenario Comparison
Given: Invest $3M at $10M pre-money ($1.43/share, 7M shares) | 3-year hold

Scenario A — M&A at $20M (2×):
  MOIC = $20M ÷ $10M = 2.0×
  IRR = (2.0)^(1÷3) − 1 = 25.99% ≈ 26%

Scenario B — IPO at $50M (5×):
  MOIC = $50M ÷ $10M = 5.0×
  IRR = (5.0)^(1÷3) − 1 = 70.6%

Scenario C — Secondary sale at $15M (1.5×) after Year 2:
  Partial exit = sell 30% of shares at $15M → $4.5M proceeds
  Remaining stake at $15M pre-money = 70% × $15M = $10.5M value
  Total value = $4.5M + $10.5M = $15M (same multiple, but got $4.5M cash earlier)

With liquidation preference (1× non-participating, $5M investment, 30% equity):
  Exit $10M: Investor gets Max($5M, $3M) = $5M (pref wins) | Founder = $5M
  Exit $20M: Investor gets Max($5M, $6M) = $6M (pro-rata wins) | Founder = $14M
