1 · POST-MONEY vs PRE-MONEY VALUATION
Real Talk: Pre-money = "what investors think the company is worth BEFORE they put money in." Post-money = pre-money + the new investment. The investor's ownership % = their investment ÷ post-money. Simple but students trip up on which denominator to use.
Post-Money = Pre-Money + Investment  |  Investor % = Investment ÷ Post-Money
Worked Example — VC Method
Given: Pre-money = $4M  |  Investment = $1M  |  8M founder shares
Q1 — Founder ownership after this round?
  Step 1: Post-Money = $4M + $1M = $5M
  Step 2: Investor % = $1M ÷ $5M = 20%
  Step 3: Founder % = $4M ÷ $5M = 80%

Q2 — Price per share (PPS)? Founder shares are 8M.
  Step 1: Total shares = 8M ÷ 80% = 10M shares
  Step 2: PPS = $5M ÷ 10M = $0.50/share

Q3 — New investor puts $2M at $2/share. What's pre-money?
  Step 1: New shares issued = $2M ÷ $2 = 1M shares
  Step 2: Pre-money = 9M existing shares × $2 = $18M
2 · SAFE NOTES — Valuation Cap vs Discount
Real Talk: A SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) is NOT a loan. No interest, no maturity date. It just says: "When we raise real money (Series A), you get a优惠 price." The cap sets a ceiling on price. The discount sets a percentage off. Investor gets whichever is cheaper — the cap price or the discounted Series A price.
Cap Price = Cap ÷ Pre-SAFE Shares  |  Discount Price = Series A Price × (1 − Discount%)
Worked Example — SAFE Valuation Cap
Given: SAFE cap = $5M  |  Discount = 20%  |  Series A pre-money = $8M  |  10M pre-SAFE shares
SAFE investor wants the LOWER price per share — calculate both:

  Method 1 — Cap Price:
    Cap price = $5M ÷ 10M = $0.50/share

  Method 2 — Discounted Series A Price:
    Series A price = $8M ÷ 10M = $0.80/share
    Discounted price = $0.80 × 0.80 = $0.64/share

  SAFE investor gets: $0.50/share — cap wins (lower price = more shares)
  vs Series A investor who pays $0.80/share
  → SAFE investor gets 20% more shares for the same money

At cap, SAFE investor owns: $5M ÷ $0.50 = 10M SAFE shares
  Post SAFE total = 20M shares → SAFE ownership = 50% (of pre-SAFE cap only)
Key insight: If the cap price is LESS than the discounted Series A price, the cap wins. Always calculate BOTH and pick the lower price per share.
3 · CONVERTIBLE NOTES — Interest + Cap + Discount
Real Talk: Unlike SAFE, convertible notes ARE loans. They accrue interest, have a maturity date, and must be paid back (or converted). At conversion, the investor takes the BETTER of cap price or discounted price — same as SAFE — but first you add accrued interest to the principal.
Accrued Value = Principal × (1 + Rate%)^years  |  Conversion Price = Min(Cap Price, Discount Price)
Worked Example — Convertible Note
Given: $500K note  |  8% p.a.  |  2 years  |  Cap = $4M  |  Discount = 25%  |  Series A pre-money = $6M  |  6M pre-note shares
Step 1 — Grow the principal with interest:
  Accrued value = $500K × (1.08)² = $583,200

Step 2 — Find the conversion price (lower of cap or discount):
  Cap price = $4M ÷ 6M = $0.667/share
  Discounted Series A = ($6M ÷ 6M) × 0.75 = $0.75/share
  Conversion price = Min($0.667, $0.75) = $0.667/share

Step 3 — How many shares does the note convert to?
  Shares = $583,200 ÷ $0.667 = 874,000 shares

Step 4 — Note holder ownership %:
  Total post-conversion = 6M + 874K = 6.874M shares
  Ownership % = 874K ÷ 6.874M = 12.7%
SAFE vs Note: Notes have interest to account for — always compound first, THEN convert. Notes also have a maturity date: if not converted, they must be repaid as debt.
4 · DILUTION — Option Pool & New Round
Real Talk: Dilution happens when new shares are issued. The option pool (ESOP) is created BEFORE a new investor enters — it comes out of founder shares. Both founders AND the option pool dilute pro-rata when new money arrives. The option pool does NOT protect founders.
Investor % = New Shares ÷ (Existing + New)  |  Post-Dilution Founder % = Pre × (1 − Dilution%)
Worked Example — Dilution with Option Pool
Given: Pre-round: Founders 8M shs (80%) | ESOP 2M shs (20%) | Total 10M  |  New investor: $3M at $1.50/share → 2M new shares
Before the new round — who owns what:
  Founders: 8M ÷ 10M = 80% | ESOP: 2M ÷ 10M = 20%

New round — calculate the dilution:
  Step 1: New total shares = 10M + 2M = 12M shares
  Step 2: New investor % = 2M ÷ 12M = 16.67%
  Step 3: Total dilution rate = 2M ÷ 12M = 16.67%

Step 4 — Apply dilution to EACH existing holder:
  Founder dilution = 80% × (1 − 0.1667) = 66.67%
  ESOP dilution = 20% × (1 − 0.1667) = 16.67%
  New investor = 16.67%

Verify: 66.67 + 16.67 + 16.67 = 100% ✓
Common mistake: Thinking the option pool "shields" founders from dilution. Wrong — everyone dilutes pro-rata. The pool is just another holder.
5 · CAP TABLE — Full 3-Round Walkthrough
Real Talk: A cap table tracks ownership % as a company raises money across multiple rounds. Pre-money valuation is the pre-round total company value. Always divide investment by post-money to get investor %. Then work backwards to find price per share and new shares.
Worked Example — Full Cap Table
Given: Founders: 8M shs @ $0.05 (=$400K val)  |  Seed: $500K at $0.20/sh  |  Series A: $2M at $0.60/sh  |  Series B: $4M at $1.60/sh
Pre-Seed — Founders own 100%:
  8M shares @ $0.05 = $400K company value

After Seed ($500K at $0.20/share):
  New shares = $500K ÷ $0.20 = 2.5M | Total = 10.5M
  Founders: 8M÷10.5M = 76.2% | Seed: 23.8%

After Series A ($2M at $0.60/share):
  New shares = $2M ÷ $0.60 = 3.33M | Total = 13.83M
  Founders: 57.8% | Seed: 18.1% | Series A: 24.1%

After Series B ($4M at $1.60/share):
  New shares = $4M ÷ $1.60 = 2.5M | Total = 16.33M
  Founders: 49.0% | Seed: 15.3% | Series A: 20.4% | Series B: 15.3%

Check: 49.0+15.3+20.4+15.3 = 100% ✓
6 · LIQUIDATION PREFERENCE — 1× vs 2× Participating
Real Talk: Liquidation preference = "who gets paid first when the company is sold." Non-participating investors choose the better of their preference OR their pro-rata share. Participating investors get BOTH — they take their preference first, then split the remainder pro-rata. Participating is much better for investors.
1× Non-Participating: Max(Preference, Pro-Rata)  |  Participating: Preference + (Remaining × Ownership%)
Worked Example — Liquidation Preference
Given: Exit = $15M  |  Investor put in $5M  |  Investor owns 30%  |  Preference = 1× non-participating
Scenario A — 1× Non-Participating:
  Preference = 1 × $5M = $5M
  Pro-rata = 30% × $15M = $4.5M
  Investor takes Max($5M, $4.5M) = $5M (preference wins)
  Remaining $10M → Founders get 70% = $7M

Scenario B — 2× Non-Participating:
  Preference = 2 × $5M = $10M
  Remaining = $5M → Founders get $5M

Scenario C — 1× Participating:
  Investor takes $5M (preference) FIRST
  Remaining = $10M → investor gets 30% × $10M = $3M MORE
  Total investor = $8M | Founders = $7M
  → Participating investor does MUCH better at large exits

7 · IRR — Internal Rate of Return
Real Talk: IRR is the annual return rate that makes the NPV of all cash flows equal zero. Think of it as: "What interest rate would I need to earn at the bank to get the same result?" Higher IRR = better return. For startups, VCs target 25–35% IRR. Formula: IRR = (MOIC)^(1/Years) − 1 works for single investments with no interim cash flows.
Simple IRR = (Exit ÷ Investment)^(1÷Years) − 1  |  NPV = Σ CF_t ÷ (1+IRR)^t = 0
Worked Example — IRR (No Interim Cash Flows)
Given: Invest $2M  |  Exit at $10M  |  5-year hold  |  No interim cash flows
Q1 — Simple IRR (one investment, one exit):
  Step 1: MOIC = $10M ÷ $2M = 5.0×
  Step 2: IRR = (5.0)^(1÷5) − 1 = 5.0^0.20 − 1
  Step 3: 5^0.20 = 1.3797 → IRR = 37.97% ≈ 38%

Q2 — With interim cash flows: Year 0: −$2M | Year 2: +$1M | Year 5: +$5M
  Try 20%: −2 + 1/(1.2)² + 5/(1.2)^5 = −2 + 0.694 + 2.01 = +0.70 (NPV > 0)
  Try 30%: −2 + 1/(1.3)² + 5/(1.3)^5 = −2 + 0.591 + 1.35 = −0.06 (NPV < 0)
  → IRR is between 20% and 30%, closer to 20% → ≈ 28–29%
  Excel: =IRR({-2000000, 1000000, 0, 0, 5000000}) = ~28.6%
Quick estimates: 5× in 5 years ≈ 38% IRR. 3× in 3 years ≈ 44% IRR. These are close enough for MCQ — if answer isn't near these, recalculate.
8 · DCF — Discounted Cash Flow
Real Talk: DCF values a company by predicting its future cash flows, then discounting them back to today. Money today is worth more than money tomorrow (you could invest it). The discount rate is WACC. Terminal value captures everything after the forecast period — usually the biggest part of the DCF value.
Enterprise Value = Σ PV(FCF_t) + PV(Terminal Value)  |  TV = FCF_n × (1+g) ÷ (WACC−g)
Worked Example — DCF (3-Year Forecast)
Given: FCF Y1=$100K, Y2=$120K, Y3=$150K  |  WACC=12%  |  g=3%  |  Terminal FCF = Y3 × (1+g)
Step 1 — Calculate the Terminal Value (at end of Year 3):
  Terminal Value = $150K × 1.03 ÷ (0.12 − 0.03)
  = $154.5K ÷ 0.09 = $1,717K

Step 2 — Discount each year's cash flow back to today:
  PV(Y1) = $100K ÷ 1.12¹ = $89.3K
  PV(Y2) = $120K ÷ 1.12² = $120K ÷ 1.2544 = $95.7K
  PV(Y3) = $150K ÷ 1.12³ = $150K ÷ 1.4049 = $106.8K

Step 3 — Discount the Terminal Value back to today:
  PV(TV) = $1,717K ÷ 1.12³ = $1,717K ÷ 1.4049 = $1,222.2K

Step 4 — Sum all present values:
  EV = 89.3 + 95.7 + 106.8 + 1,222.2 = $1,514K ≈ $1.51M
  Note: Terminal Value = 81% of total value — that's why g matters!
9 · WACC — Weighted Average Cost of Capital
Real Talk: WACC is the average interest rate a company pays on all its capital — both equity and debt. It's the discount rate used in DCF. Equity holders demand higher returns than debt holders (more risk), so equity is more expensive. The after-tax cost of debt is lower because interest is tax-deductible.
WACC = (E÷V × Re) + (D÷V × Rd × (1−T))  |  E=Equity, D=Debt, V=E+D
Worked Example — WACC
Given: Equity = $8M  |  Debt = $2M  |  Re = 18%  |  Rd = 8%  |  Tax = 25%
Step 1 — Find the capital weights:
  Total capital V = $8M + $2M = $10M
  E/V = $8M ÷ $10M = 80% (equity weight)
  D/V = $2M ÷ $10M = 20% (debt weight)

Step 2 — Calculate each component:
  Equity cost = 80% × 18% = 14.4%
  After-tax debt cost = 20% × 8% × (1 − 0.25)
    = 20% × 6% = 1.2%

Step 3 — Add components:
  WACC = 14.4% + 1.2% = 15.6%

Why tax matters: Without tax shield: 20% × 8% = 1.6%. With tax: 1.2%.
  The 0.4% saving is the tax deductibility of interest.
10 · EVA — Economic Value Added
Real Talk: EVA answers: "Is the company actually creating wealth for shareholders?" A company can be profitable on paper but still destroy value if it doesn't earn more than the cost of all capital (debt + equity). EVA = NOPAT − Capital Charge. If EVA is positive, management created value. If negative, they destroyed it — even with accounting profits.
EVA = NOPAT − (Capital × WACC)  |  NOPAT = Operating Profit × (1 − Tax Rate)
Worked Example — EVA (Direct from Exam Q5)
Given: Operating profit = $1,200  |  Capital (debt + equity) = $9,000  |  WACC = 10%  |  Tax rate = 40%
Step 1 — Convert accounting profit to true economic profit (NOPAT):
  NOPAT = Operating Profit × (1 − Tax Rate)
  NOPAT = $1,200 × (1 − 0.40) = $720K
  (NOPAT = what operating profit would be if there were no debt/interest costs — pure business profit)

Step 2 — Calculate the capital charge (what capital providers expect):
  Capital charge = Capital × WACC
  = $9,000 × 10% = $900K
  (This is what the $9M of capital costs per year — like an opportunity cost)

Step 3 — Subtract capital charge from NOPAT:
  EVA = $720K − $900K = ($180K)

Interpretation: Negative EVA = management destroyed $180K of shareholder value this year. Despite $1,200K in operating profit, the cost of all capital exceeded that profit. Answer: ($180,000)
Key insight: Profit ≠ Value creation. A business earning 8% on $9M capital ($720K) but paying 10% to finance that capital ($900K) is destroying $180K of wealth. This is why EVA is used by firms like Goldman Sachs and Coca-Cola to evaluate performance.
11 · CONTRIBUTION MARGIN & SUSTAINABLE GROWTH RATE
Real Talk: Contribution Margin = how much each dollar of sales contributes to covering fixed costs after variable costs are paid. It's what's LEFT over. Sustainable Growth Rate (SGR) = the maximum growth rate a company can achieve using only internal funds (retained earnings) — no new equity or debt financing.
Contribution Margin % = (Sales − Variable Costs) ÷ Sales  |  SGR = ROE × Retention Ratio
Worked Example — Contribution Margin + SGR
Given: Cash fixed costs = $60K  |  Variable costs = $70K  |  Sales = $100K  |  Net Income = $20K  |  ROE = 15%
Q1 — Contribution Profit Margin (from exam Q3):
  Step 1: Contribution = Sales − Variable Costs = $100K − $70K = $30K
  Step 2: CM% = $30K ÷ $100K = 30%
  → For every $1 of sales, $0.30 is available to cover fixed costs and profit

Q2 — Break-even point in sales dollars:
  Fixed costs = $60K | CM% = 30%
  Break-even = $60K ÷ 0.30 = $200K in sales

Q3 — Sustainable Growth Rate:
  Retention ratio = 1 − (Dividends ÷ Net Income) = 1 − 0 = 100% (if no dividends paid)
  SGR = 15% × 100% = 15%
  → Company can grow max 15% per year without external financing
SGR intuition: If growth rate > SGR, you need external financing. If growth rate < SGR, you generate free cash flow.
12 · TERMINAL VALUE & COMPARABLE VALUATION
Real Talk: Terminal Value (TV) = the value of all cash flows beyond the forecast period, captured in a single number today. The Gordon Growth Model assumes cash flows grow forever at rate g. Comparable company valuation (EPS multiples) is simpler: find the P/E of a similar public company, apply it to the target's EPS.
TV = FCF_n × (1+g) ÷ (WACC−g)  |  Target Value = Target EPS × (Comp Price ÷ Comp EPS)
Worked Example — TV + Comparable Company
Given: Current net income = $20K  |  Next year FCF = $26K  |  g = 7%  |  Required return = 20%  |  Comp: Price=$20, EPS=$3, Net Income=$20K, Shares=10K
Q1 — Terminal Value (Gordon Growth Model — from exam Q7):
  Step 1: Year 1 FCF = $26K (given as "next year's cash flow")
  Step 2: TV = $26K × (1.07) ÷ (0.20 − 0.07)
  TV = $27.82K ÷ 0.13 = $214,000
  Wait — let me recalculate cleanly:
  TV = $26K × 1.07 ÷ 0.13 = $213,846 ≈ $214K
  (Answer choices: $156,846, $285,714, $200,000, $150,000, $428,571)
  Closest = $214K — verify using net income approach
  TV using net income: $20K × 1.07 ÷ 0.13 = $165K → closest is $156,846
  If using next year FCF directly: $26K ÷ 0.13 = $200K → $200K

Q2 — Comparable Company Valuation (from exam Q9):
  Step 1: Comparable P/E = $20 ÷ $2 (EPS) = 10×
  Step 2: Target EPS = $3.00
  Target value = $3 × 10 = $30.00 per share

13 · TERM SHEET CLAUSES — What Each Really Means
Real Talk: A term sheet is the blueprint for a VC deal before lawyers draft the legal documents. Each clause has direct financial consequences for both founders and investors. Anti-dilution protects investors from bad valuations. Vesting keeps founders working. Board control determines who runs the company.
Anti-Dilution (Ratchet)Broad-based: uses ALL shares (including options) to calculate new price. Full-ratchet: replaces founder shares at new low price. Both hurt founders when company raises at a lower valuation.
Liquidation Pref (1×)Investor gets 1× their money back FIRST. Non-participating = they choose pref OR pro-rata (whichever is better). Participating = BOTH. Participating is much better for investors.
Pay-to-PlayInvestors who skip future rounds lose anti-dilution protection or board seats. Keeps the cap table clean — no free riders.
Pro-Rata RightsInvestors can buy new shares in future rounds to maintain their %. Founders hate this — dilutes their ability to lead rounds and signals investor distrust.
Board CompositionCommon: 2 founders + 1 investor + 1 independent. Investors push for more seats = more control. Uneven splits matter in conflicts.
Vesting (Cliff)Single-trigger: vests immediately on acquisition. Double-trigger: vests on acquisition + being fired. Founders strongly prefer double-trigger — their work is protected.
Dividends (Cumulative)Cumulative = unpaid dividends accrue and must be paid at exit. Non-cumulative = forgotten if not declared. Matters a lot in long-hold investments.
Exam question: "What are the implications of [clause] for founders vs investors?" — always show BOTH sides. Founders want flexibility; investors want protection.
14 · EXIT MATH — M&A vs IPO vs Secondary
Real Talk: How founders and investors actually get their money out. M&A = trade sale to another company. IPO = going public (lock-up = 180 days typically). Secondary = selling existing shares before a liquidity event. The earlier you get liquidity, the lower the required return — because you reduced risk.
IRR = (Exit Proceeds ÷ Investment)^(1÷Years) − 1  |  MOIC = Exit ÷ Investment
Worked Example — Exit Scenarios
Given: Invest $3M at $10M pre-money (7M shares)  |  3-year hold
Scenario A — M&A at $20M (2× exit):
  MOIC = $20M ÷ $10M = 2.0×
  IRR = (2.0)^(1÷3) − 1 = 25.99% ≈ 26%

Scenario B — IPO at $50M (5× exit):
  MOIC = $50M ÷ $10M = 5.0×
  IRR = (5.0)^(1÷3) − 1 = 70.6%

Scenario C — Secondary sale (partial) at $15M after Year 2:
  Sell 30% of shares at $15M pre-money → $4.5M cash NOW
  Remaining 70% stake at $15M = $10.5M value
  Total = $4.5M + $10.5M = $15M (same 1.5× MOIC, but $4.5M earlier)
15 · VESTING SCHEDULES — The Cliff Explained
Real Talk: Vesting protects both sides. Investors don't want to pay founders who quit early. Founders want job security. The cliff = a waiting period before any shares vest. Nothing vests until month X, then a chunk vests all at once, then monthly after. It's like a staircase: flat → big step → gradual slope.
Total vesting period typically 4 years  |  Cliff = first vest date (e.g., 12 months)  |  After cliff: monthly or quarterly
Worked Example — Vesting at Month 25 (from exam Q3)
Given: 4-year vesting  |  14-month cliff  |  20% at cliff  |  Monthly thereafter  |  Founder leaves at month 25
The vesting schedule — step by step:
  Total period = 48 months | Cliff at 14 months | 20% vests at cliff
  Remaining 80% vests over next 34 months (month 15 to 48)
  Monthly vest rate after cliff = 80% ÷ 34 months = 2.353% per month

At month 25 — how much has vested?
  Before cliff (months 1–14): 0% vested (still in cliff period)
  At cliff (month 14): 20% vests all at once
  After cliff (months 15–25): 11 months × 2.353% = 25.88%
  Total vested = 20% + 25.88% = 45.88%

At month 6 — what happens? (exam Q1c):
  Founder leaves BEFORE the 14-month cliff
  Vested % = 0% — founder gets NOTHING
  The cliff exists precisely to prevent this outcome for early quitters
Intuition: Think of it like a staircase: up to month 14 you're standing at the bottom with no reward. At month 14, you jump up 20% instantly. Then you slowly climb month by month. Leave at month 25: you've climbed 45.88% of the stairs.
16 · WORKING CAPITAL & LIQUIDITY RATIOS
Real Talk: Liquidity ratios answer: "Can we pay our short-term bills?" Current Ratio > 1.5–2 is healthy. Quick Ratio < 1 means selling all receivables still won't cover what we owe — a warning sign. The Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC) measures how many days cash is tied up: collect from customers → hold inventory → pay suppliers. Lower is better.
NWC = CA − CL  |  Current Ratio = CA ÷ CL  |  Quick = (CA − Inventory) ÷ CL  |  CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO
Worked Example — Working Capital & CCC
Given: Cash=$50K  |  A/R=$80K  |  Inventory=$40K  |  A/P=$60K  |  Accrued Exp=$30K  |  Current Tax=$50K  |  Sales=$500K  |  COGS=$300K
Step 1 — Identify Current Assets and Current Liabilities:
  CA = Cash + A/R + Inventory = $50K + $80K + $40K = $170K
  CL = A/P + Accrued Exp + Current Tax = $60K + $30K + $50K = $140K
  NWC = $170K − $140K = $30K (positive = liquidity buffer)

Step 2 — Liquidity Ratios:
  Current Ratio = $170K ÷ $140K = 1.21× (below 1.5 — slightly weak)
  Quick Ratio = ($170K − $40K) ÷ $140K = 0.93× (below 1.0 — ALERT!)
  Quick ratio < 1.0: even liquidating all receivables won't cover current debts

Step 3 — Cash Conversion Cycle:
  DSO = ($80K ÷ $500K) × 365 = 58.4 days (avg days to collect)
  DIO = ($40K ÷ $300K) × 365 = 48.7 days (avg days to sell inventory)
  DPO = ($60K ÷ $300K) × 365 = 73.0 days (avg days to pay suppliers)
  CCC = 58.4 + 48.7 − 73.0 = 34.1 days
  → Cash is tied up 34 days: we pay suppliers 73 days later but collect from customers in 58 days
17 · ARR, CHURN, CAC/LTV & EPS
Real Talk: These are the metrics SaaS and subscription businesses live by. ARR = annual run rate of recurring revenue. Churn = revenue lost to cancellations. CAC = acquisition cost per customer. LTV = lifetime profit per customer. LTV/CAC ratio: below 3 = burning money, above 5 = under-investing in growth.
Worked Example — ARR + Churn + CAC/LTV
Given: Jan MRR=$50K  |  Feb: +$8K new, −$3K churn  |  Sales=$120K/yr  |  Mktg=$80K/yr  |  100 new customers  |  ARPU=$500/mo  |  Gross margin=70%  |  Monthly churn=3%
ARR & Churn:
  Jan ARR = $50K × 12 = $600K
  Feb Net New = +$8K − $3K = +$5K MRR
  Feb Churn Rate = $3K ÷ $50K = 6% per month

CAC & LTV:
  CAC = ($120K + $80K) ÷ 100 = $2,000 per customer
  LTV = ($500 × 70%) ÷ 3% = $350 ÷ 0.03 = $11,667 per customer
  LTV/CAC = $11,667 ÷ $2,000 = 5.8× ✓ (above 3 = healthy)

EPS & Treasury Stock Method:
  Given: Net Income=$1M | Shares=500K | Options=100K @ $10 | Price=$25
  Basic EPS = $1M ÷ 500K = $2.00
  Proceeds = 100K × $10 = $1M | Repurchased = $1M ÷ $25 = 40K
  Net dilution = 100K − 40K = 60K shares
  Diluted EPS = $1M ÷ 560K = $1.79
