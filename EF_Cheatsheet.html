1. Working Capital & Liquidity Ratios

Net Working Capital = how many dollars of liquid assets you have left after paying all short-term bills. If it's negative, you're one bad month away from missing payroll.
NWC = (Cash + A/R + Inventory) − (A/P + Accrued Expenses + Current Tax Payable)
Current Ratio measures whether you have enough total current assets to cover every current liability — a ratio below 1.0 means you're technically insolvent on a short-term basis.
Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
Quick Ratio strips inventory because you can't always sell it fast enough to pay a bill tomorrow — it's the "if we got cut off today, could we survive?" ratio.
Quick Ratio = (Cash + A/R) / Current Liabilities
Cash Ratio = Cash / Current Liabilities   (most conservative — cash only)
Worked Example — Liquidity Analysis
GIVENS:
Cash = $50K    A/R = $80K    Inventory = $40K
A/P = $60K    Accrued Expenses = $30K    Current Tax = $50K
Sales = $500K    COGS = $300K
Step 1 — NWC: CA = 50 + 80 + 40 = $170K  |  CL = 60 + 30 + 50 = $140K  |  NWC = 170 − 140 = $30K
Step 2 — Current Ratio: 170 / 140 = 1.21x
Step 3 — Quick Ratio: (50 + 80) / 140 = 130 / 140 = 0.93x  (inventory excluded)
Step 4 — Cash Ratio: 50 / 140 = 0.36x
NWC = $30K  |  Current Ratio = 1.21x  |  Quick Ratio = 0.93x  |  Cash Ratio = 0.36x
Quick Ratio < 1.0 means inventory is doing a lot of heavy lifting. Here, 0.93 means you're $10K short of covering all liabilities without selling inventory — a red flag for lenders.

2. Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)

The CCC answers: "How many days does it take from paying for raw materials to collecting from customers?" Fewer days = healthier cash flow. If your CCC is 60 days and a competitor runs it in 30, they're effectively getting a 30-day free loan on every dollar of working capital.
DSO = (A/R / Sales) × 365     DIO = (Inventory / COGS) × 365     DPO = (A/P / COGS) × 365
CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO
Worked Example — Cash Conversion Cycle
GIVENS: A/R = $80K  |  Inventory = $40K  |  A/P = $60K  |  Sales = $500K  |  COGS = $300K
Step 1 — DSO (Days Sales Outstanding): (80 / 500) × 365 = 0.16 × 365 = 58.4 days
Step 2 — DIO (Days Inventory Outstanding): (40 / 300) × 365 = 0.1333 × 365 = 48.7 days
Step 3 — DPO (Days Payable Outstanding): (60 / 300) × 365 = 0.20 × 365 = 73.0 days
Step 4 — CCC: 58.4 + 48.7 − 73.0 = 34.1 days
DSO = 58.4d  |  DIO = 48.7d  |  DPO = 73.0d  |  CCC = 34.1 days
DPO > DSO is ideal — you're stretching out what you owe while collecting faster than you restock. Here, DPO (73d) exceeds DIO (49d) by 24 days, a sign of strong payables management.

3. Vesting Schedules

Vesting aligns founder/employee incentives with the company's long-term survival. The cliff exists so nobody can quit in month 2 and walk away with 16.7% of the company. After the cliff, equity accumulates monthly — you earn it a little bit every day you're still there.
Monthly vesting rate (after cliff) = (100% − Cliff%) / (Total Months − Cliff Month)
Alternatively: Cliff% + (Months After Cliff × Post-Cliff Monthly Rate)
Vesting — Founder Leaves at Month 25 (4-Year / 14-Month Cliff / 20% Cliff)
GIVENS: 4-year total (48 months)  |  14-month cliff  |  20% vests at cliff  |  monthly thereafter
Step 1 — Cliff check: Month 25 > Month 14 → cliff has been crossed → 20% cliff shares are vested
Step 2 — Monthly rate after cliff: 20% / 34 remaining months = 0.5882% per month
Step 3 — Months after cliff: 25 − 14 = 11 months of additional vesting
Step 4 — Post-cliff vesting: 11 × 0.5882% = 6.47%
Step 5 — Total: 20.00% + 6.47% = 26.47%  (confirms: 20% + 11×(20%/34) = 26.47%)
Vested at Month 25 = 26.47%
Vesting — Founder Leaves at Month 6 (Before Cliff)
Cliff check: Month 6 < Month 14 → cliff NOT yet reached
Vested at Month 6 = 0% — founder forfeits all equity
The monthly rate is calculated on the REMAINING pool after the cliff: (100% − 20%) / (48 − 14) = 2.353% per month if expressed as cumulative monthly rate. Note: some texts express this as a flat monthly % of the original grant (20%/48 = 0.4167%), which yields different answers — use the post-cliff method above for cliffed schedules.

4. Exit Math: M&A vs IPO vs Secondary

In a liquidity event, everyone's payout depends on two things: the exit enterprise value and where you sit in the cap table. The liquidation waterfall determines whether your equity is worth the paper it's printed on.
Equity Value at Exit = Exit EV − Net Debt − Preferred Liquidation Preference
Your Proceeds = Equity Value × Your Ownership %     ROI = (Proceeds / Investment) − 1
MOIC = Proceeds / Investment     IRR solves: Σ(Cash Flow_t / (1+IRR)^t) = 0
M&A Exit — $50M Acquisition, 4 Years, 1.5x Liquidation Pref
Exit EV = $50M  |  Net Debt = $10M  |  Series A liquidation pref = 1.5x  |  Series A investment = $5M  |  Founder common = 40%
Step 1 — Liquidation preference: $5M × 1.5x = $7.5M (Series A gets paid first)
Step 2 — Remaining after pref: $50M − $7.5M = $42.5M (common equity pool)
Step 3 — Founder proceeds (common): $42.5M × 40% = $17.0M
Step 4 — Series A total proceeds: $7.5M (pref) + $42.5M × 60% = $7.5M + $25.5M = $33.0M
Step 5 — Series A ROI: ($33M / $5M) − 1 = 5.6x  |  IRR ≈ 53.7%
Founder: $17.0M  |  Series A: $33.0M  |  Series A ROI: 5.6x / 53.7% IRR
IPO vs M&A — Impact of Structure
Company exit = $100M  |  Preferred stock = $30M (1.0x non-participating)  |  Common = 70%
M&A (liquidation pref triggers): Pref gets $30M + common pool = $30M + $70M = $100M total → common gets $0
IPO (no liquidation pref, direct listing): All equity participates at market → common value = $100M × 70% = $70M
M&A common: $0  |  IPO common: $70M — structure matters enormously
Participating preferred (Dividends accruing + participation in common) can dramatically reduce common-founder proceeds in M&A. Always read whether the preferred "participates" after receiving its preference.

6. EPS & Treasury Stock Method

EPS tells you how much profit each share of common stock "owns." Diluted EPS goes further and assumes every option, warrant, and convertible security has been exercised — because in a real exit, they all convert and dilute common shareholders.
Basic EPS = (Net Income − Preferred Dividends) / Weighted Average Common Shares Outstanding
Diluted EPS = (Net Income − Preferred Dividends) / (Weighted Avg Shares + Net New Shares from TS Method)
Treasury Stock Method (for options/warrants):
Step 1: Assume all in-the-money options exercised at average exercise price
Step 2: Assume proceeds used to buy back shares at average market price
Step 3: Net new shares = Options Exercised − Shares Repurchased
Dilutive shares = ((Exercise Price / Market Price) × Options) < Options — always net-positive
EPS — Basic vs Diluted
Net Income = $2M  |  Preferred Dividends = $200K  |  Avg Common Shares = 800,000
In-the-money options: 100,000 shares  |  Avg exercise price = $20  |  Avg market price = $50
Step 1 — Basic EPS: ($2M − $200K) / 800K = $1.8M / 800K = $2.25 per share
Step 2 — TS Method: Proceeds = 100K × $20 = $2M  |  Shares repurchased = $2M / $50 = 40K shares
Step 3 — Net dilutive shares: 100K − 40K = 60,000 net new shares
Step 4 — Diluted EPS: $1.8M / (800K + 60K) = $1.8M / 860K = $2.09 per share
Basic EPS = $2.25  |  Diluted EPS = $2.09  |  Dilution = 7.1%
Dilutive Convertible Notes
$5M convertible note, converts to 200,000 shares at $25/share  |  Market price at conversion = $40/share
If-converted method: Assume conversion at issuance → adds 200K shares to denominator immediately
Treasury stock method check: 200K shares × ($40 − $25) / $40 = 200K × 0.375 = 75K net dilutive shares
Rule: Use whichever method gives HIGHER EPS (less dilution) — but for full dilution use if-converted
Convertible note dilutes by 200K shares (if-converted) — check if anti-dilutive first
Anti-dilutive securities are EXCLUDED from diluted EPS. If exercise price > average market price, the option is out-of-the-money and adds zero dilution. Always check: is the option in or out of the money before calculating.

7. Post-Money vs Pre-Money Valuation (VC Method)

Pre-money valuation is what the company is worth before the investor writes a check. Post-money is what it's worth after — the pre-money plus the fresh capital. The VC Method works backwards from an ambitious exit valuation to tell you what the company is worth today. The trap: mixing up which number goes where, or forgetting that the option pool lives inside the pre-money (which dilutes founders more than VCs expect).
POST-MONEY = PRE-MONEY + Investment  |  PRE-MONEY = POST-MONEY − Investment
VC Method: Pre-money = Exit Value / (1+Target IRR)n
Ownership% = Investment / Post-money
Worked Example — VC Method (FNCE696 Style)
Angel invests $500K for 20% equity  |  Target exit: $20M in Year 5  |  Target IRR: 40%  |  Option pool: 10% post-money
Step 1 — Find Post-money from ownership stake: Post-money = Investment / Ownership% = $500K / 20% = $2,500,000
Step 2 — Back out Pre-money (before the $500K): Pre-money = Post-money − Investment = $2.5M − $500K = $2,000,000
Step 3 — VC Method (present value of exit): PV = $20M / (1.40)5 = $20M / 5.3783 = $3,719,000
Step 4 — Check implied ownership at VC Method price: $500K / $3.72M = 13.4% (not 20% — the VC Method produces a higher valuation)
Post-money = $2,500,000  |  Pre-money = $2,000,000  |  VC Method implied pre-money = $3,719,000
Exam Trick: When you see "option pool" mentioned, ask — is it pre-money or post-money? YC and most VCs require the option pool inside pre-money, which means founders absorb that dilution, not the incoming investor.

8. SAFE Notes (Valuation Cap vs Discount)

A SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) is Y Combinator's lightweight alternative to a convertible note — it is not debt, has no interest rate, no maturity date, and no repayment obligation. The founder wins because there's no countdown clock. The investor wins because they get either a discount or a valuation cap — whichever gives them more shares. The math contest: cap beats discount when the round is at a high enough valuation that the cap-price is below the discounted round price.
SAFE conversion price = MIN(Cap / Pre-money shares, Round price × (1−Discount))
Shares received = SAFE amount / Conversion price  |  Discount price = Round price × (1−d)
Cap price = Cap / Pre-money shares outstanding
Worked Example — SAFE: Cap vs Discount Showdown
SAFE amount = $200,000  |  Valuation cap = $3,000,000  |  Discount = 20%  |  Series A pre-money = $8,000,000  |  Existing shares = 4,000,000 (founders)  |  New option pool = 1,000,000
Step 1 — Round price per share: Total shares = 4M + 1M = 5M  |  Round price = $8M / 5M = $1.60/share
Step 2 — Path A (Discount): Discount price = $1.60 × 0.80 = $1.28/share
Step 2 — Path B (Cap): Cap price = $3M / 4M shares = $0.75/share
Step 3 — Pick LOWER price (more shares): MIN($1.28, $0.75) = $0.75/share — Cap wins
Step 4 — SAFE shares received: $200K / $0.75 = 266,667 shares
Step 5 — Ownership %: 266,667 / (5M + 266,667) = 5.06%
SAFE conversion price = $0.75/share (cap wins)  |  Shares = 266,667  |  Ownership = 5.06%
Key Insight: SAFE caps reference pre-money shares (excluding the new option pool). A $3M cap on 4M founder shares = $0.75/share equivalent. The Series A investor pays $1.60/share. The SAFE investor saves $0.85/share — this is the economic benefit of the cap.
Common Mistake: Dividing the cap by post-option-pool shares is wrong! SAFE caps always reference pre-money shares at the time of conversion.

9. Convertible Notes (Interest + Cap + Discount)

Convertible notes are actual debt — they accrue interest and have a maturity date when they must be repaid (or converted). Unlike SAFEs, the interest earned compounds the principal, so the investor effectively converts more shares at the next round. The cap and discount stack: the note holder gets whichever produces more shares — the lower of (cap-hit price) vs (discounted round price). The classic founder mistake: ignoring the interest accrual, which silently inflates the conversion amount.
Accrued interest = Principal × Rate × Years  |  Total conversion amount = Principal + Accrued interest
Conversion price = MIN(Cap / Pre-money shares, Round price × (1−d))
Shares received = Total conversion amount / Conversion price
Worked Example — Convertible Note: Interest + Cap + Discount
Principal = $500,000  |  Coupon rate = 8% per annum  |  Holding period = 2 years  |  Valuation cap = $4,000,000  |  Discount = 20%  |  Series B pre-money = $15,000,000  |  Pre-money shares = 2,000,000
Step 1 — Accrued interest: $500K × 8% × 2 = $80,000
Step 2 — Total conversion amount: $500K + $80K = $580,000
Step 3 — Round price: $15M / 2M = $7.50/share
Step 4 — Path A (Discount): $7.50 × 0.80 = $6.00/share
Step 4 — Path B (Cap): $4M / 2M = $2.00/share
Step 5 — Pick LOWER price: MIN($6.00, $2.00) = $2.00/share — Cap wins decisively
Step 6 — Shares received: $580K / $2.00 = 290,000 shares
Step 7 — Effective discount vs Series B: ($7.50 − $2.00) / $7.50 = 73.3%
Accrued interest = $80,000  |  Conversion amount = $580,000  |  Price = $2.00/share  |  Shares = 290,000
Compare to SAFE: A $500K SAFE with same $4M cap and 20% discount — no interest accrual, so SAFE converts $500K (not $580K). The note holder gets 290K shares vs SAFE holder's 250K shares. The 2 years of interest bought 40,000 extra shares.
Exam Trap: Forgetting to add accrued interest to principal before computing shares = undercount. Always compound-first, then convert. Interest is typically simple annual unless stated otherwise.

10. Option Pool Shuffle

The option pool shuffle is the quietest dilution trap in startup fundraising. When VCs require a post-money option pool (e.g., "10% post-money"), they mean: AFTER the new investment, 10% of the total shares must be reserved for employees. The shuffle is WHO absorbs those shares — and the answer is always founders, not the VC.
Option pool (post-money) = New options / (Existing shares + New options + New investor shares)
Founder ownership (post-shuffle) = Founder shares / Total shares (including new pool)
VC ownership = Investment / Post-money valuation
Pre-money = Post-money − Investment  |  Post-money = Pre-money + Investment
Option Pool Shuffle — Pre-Money vs Post-Money Pool
Founders: 4,000,000 shares  |  VC invests $2M at $8M pre-money  |  VC requires 10% post-money option pool
CASE A — Option pool carved from PRE-money (founder absorbs): Pool = 10% × (4M + New shares)  |  Let New = x  |  x = 0.10 × (4M + x)  |  0.9x = 400K  |  x = 444,444 → 444,444 new options
Total shares: 4M + 444K + 2M/$8M × 4M = 4M + 444K + 1M = 5,444,444
Founder ownership: 4M / 5,444,444 = 73.5%  |  VC ownership = 1M/5.44M = 18.4%
CASE B — Option pool carved from POST-money (VC absorbs): VC buys 2M/8M = 20%  |  Post-money = $10M  |  Options = 10% × $10M = $1M equivalent  |  In shares: $1M / ($8M/4M) = 500,000 options
Total shares: 4M + 500K + 1M (VC) = 5,500,000
Founder ownership: 4M / 5.5M = 72.7%  |  VC = 1M/5.5M = 18.2%  |  slight difference
Pre-money pool (founder absorbs): founder = 73.5%, VC = 18.4%  |  Post-money pool: founder = 72.7%, VC = 18.2%
Real Talk: VCs always negotiate for a post-money pool because it comes out of the pre-money pie (i.e., from founders). Founders should push back: "We'll agree to 10% post-money if you increase the pre-money to compensate." The VC's response tells you everything about their alignment.
Key Number: Pre-money option pool = more founder dilution. Post-money option pool = same dilution to pre-money value but founders keep slightly more. Always clarify: is the option pool pre-money or post-money in the term sheet?

11. Liquidating Equity & Waterfall Analysis

In a liquidation event (M&A, bankruptcy, forced sale), everyone gets paid in a specific order. The waterfall determines how much each class of equity receives before the next class gets anything. Understanding the waterfall explains why a founder with 60% ownership might walk away with $0 in a bad M&A deal.
Waterfall order: Secured Debt → Unsecured Debt → Preferred Stock (liquidation pref) → Common Stock
Seniority stack: Participating Preferred → Non-Participating Preferred → Common
Proceeds to common = Max(0, Total Proceeds − All Preferred Preferences)
1x Non-participating: MAX(preference amount, converted common value) — picks the bigger number
Participating preferred: receives preference AND participates in remainder (double-dip)
Waterfall Analysis — $50M M&A with Multiple Rounds
Exit EV = $50M  |  Net Debt = $5M  |  Available for equity = $45M
Series A: $5M at 1x liquidation pref = $5M  |  Series B: $10M at 2x participating pref
Founder common: 8,000,000 shares  |  Employee options: 2,000,000 shares
Total fully-diluted shares: 10,000,000
Step 1 — Series A (1x non-participating): Pref = $5M  |  Check conversion: $5M vs 20% × $45M = $9M → takes $9M as common
Step 2 — Series B (2x participating): Pref = $20M  |  Remaining = $45M − $9M = $36M  |  Series B participation = 40% × $36M = $14.4M  |  Total = $20M + $14.4M = $34.4M
Step 3 — Common pool (leftover): $45M − $9M − $14.4M = $21.6M
Step 4 — Per-share price: $21.6M / 10M shares = $2.16/share
Step 5 — Option exercise check: Assume options at $0.50 exercise → in-the-money → treasury stock method applies
Series A: $9M (converted to common)  |  Series B: $34.4M  |  Common pool: $21.6M  |  Per share: $2.16
Cap Table Tip: Build the waterfall from the top down. Each class's decision (convert to common or take preference) affects what everyone below them receives. Non-participating preferred is "rich enough" to convert when the upside exceeds the preference. Participating preferred ALWAYS converts if there's any upside, because they get both the preference AND a share of the remainder.
Trap: A 2x participating preferred can actually receive MORE than 2x their investment in a large exit. Example: $10M invested, $100M exit, 50% participating → $10M (pref) + $45M (participation) = $55M total. Always model the waterfall explicitly.

12. Dilution & Cap Table Math

Cap table math is the backbone of every founder/investor negotiation. Every new round, option grant, or convertible instrument changes the ownership pie. The key insight: percentage ownership is only meaningful when you know the fully-diluted denominator.
Ownership% = Your Shares / Fully-Diluted Shares (founders + investors + options + converts)
New ownership% (after round) = Old ownership% × (Pre-money / Post-money)
Dilution from new options = New options / (Old shares + New options)
Post-money ownership = Investment / Post-money valuation
Anti-dilution (weighted avg): New price = Old price × (Old shares + New shares at new price) / (Old shares + New shares)
Cap Table Evolution — Seed to Series A
Founders: 6,000,000 shares (post-vesting)  |  Option pool: 1,000,000 (10%)  |  Seed investor: $1M at $4M pre-money
Pre-money shares: 6M (founders) + 1M (pool) = 7,000,000
Pre-money valuation: $4,000,000
Price per share: $4M / 7M = $0.5714/share
Seed investor shares: $1M / $0.5714 = 1,750,000 shares
Post-money: $4M + $1M = $5,000,000
Post-money shares: 7M + 1.75M = 8,750,000
Post-Series A ownership: Founder: 6M/8.75M = 68.6%  |  Options: 1M/8.75M = 11.4%  |  Seed: 1.75M/8.75M = 20.0%
Founder: 68.6%  |  Option pool: 11.4%  |  Seed investor: 20.0%  |  Price/share: $0.5714
Down Round — Weighted Average Anti-Dilution Adjustment
Old price = $2.00  |  Old shares = 5,000,000  |  New price = $1.00  |  New shares = 3,000,000
Broad-based weighted avg: Adjusted price = (($2.00 × 5M) + ($1.00 × 3M)) / (5M + 3M) = ($10M + $3M) / 8M = $1.625/share
Old investor receives: 5M shares × ($1.625 − $1.00) = 3,125,000 equivalent new shares worth $3.125M at new price
Adjusted exercise price = $1.625/share  |  Old investors made whole on $0.625/share of dilution
Trap: Option pool expansion at a new round = additional dilution to existing holders. If you expand the option pool from 10% to 15% at Series A, the 5% difference comes out of founders' and earlier investors' pockets, not the new investor's.

13. Vesting & Option Math (Expanded)

Beyond basic vesting schedules, real option math involves early exercise, disqualified dispositions, and the choice between ISO vs NSO taxation. The difference can be worth hundreds of thousands of dollars in taxes.
Intrinsic value = (Market price − Exercise price) × Number of options
Taxable spread (NSO/ISO AMT): FMV at exercise − Exercise price
ISO qualifying disposition: held 2 years from grant + 1 year from exercise → long-term capital gains
Disqualifying disposition (ISO): sold before holding periods met → ordinary income on spread
Early exercise (83(b)): elect within 30 days of grant → taxed at grant date FMV, not future appreciation
Option Exercise — ISO vs NSO Tax Consequences
Granted: 100,000 options at $1.00/share  |  FMV at exercise: $10.00/share  |  Sale price: $15.00/share
NSO — at exercise: Spread = ($10 − $1) × 100K = $900,000 ordinary income (taxed immediately)
NSO — at sale: Gain = ($15 − $10) × 100K = $500K capital gain (held >1yr = LTCG)
ISO qualifying — at sale (2yr/1yr met): Entire gain = ($15 − $1) × 100K = $1.4M all long-term capital gains
ISO disqualifying — sold early: Spread at exercise = $900K ordinary income  |  Remaining = $400K capital gain
Tax comparison (assume 37% + 20% FICA saved): NSO OI: $900K × 37% = $333K  |  ISO qualifying: $1.4M × 20% = $280K  |  ISO saves ~$53K
ISO qualifying: $1.4M all LTCG  |  NSO: $900K OI + $500K LTCG  |  ISO advantage: ~$53K in this scenario
83(b) Election — Early Exercise Strategy
Options: 1,000,000  |  Grant price: $0.001/share  |  FMV at grant: $0.10/share  |  83(b) election filed day 1
83(b) taxable amount: (FMV − Exercise) × Shares = ($0.10 − $0.001) × 1M = $99,000 ordinary income
Future appreciation excluded: If company exits at $10M (10M shares), FMV = $1.00/share  |  Without 83(b): $1M ordinary income at exercise  |  With 83(b): already paid $99K, rest is capital gain
83(b) savings: ($1,000,000 − $99,000) × 37% = $333,370 saved
83(b) election locks in low ordinary income tax now vs potentially much higher ordinary income at exercise
Rule of thumb: File 83(b) when the spread is small (startup at low valuation) or when you expect the FMV to appreciate dramatically before you can exercise. The 30-day window is unforgiving — missed deadline = no second chance.
AMT trap for ISOs: The spread on ISO exercise is an AMT preference item. If you exercise ISOs in a year where the spread is large and the company is doing well (high FMV), you may owe AMT even if you haven't sold the shares. Model this before large early exercises.

14. Working Capital Metrics

Working capital measures operational efficiency and short-term liquidity. Current Ratio > 1.5 is generally healthy. Quick Ratio (Acid Test) excludes inventory — more conservative. Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC) measures how fast you turn investments into cash receipts. Negative CCC = you're getting paid before you pay suppliers!
Common mistake: Thinking "profit means cash." A profitable company can still fail if it runs out of working capital to fund growth.
NWC = Current Assets - Current Liabilities
Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
Quick Ratio = (Current Assets - Inventory) / Current Liabilities
CCC = DSO + DIO - DPO [days]
DSO = (Accounts Receivable / Revenue) × 365
DIO = (Inventory / COGS) × 365
DPO = (Accounts Payable / COGS) × 365
Worked Example — Working Capital Analysis
Revenue = $365K  |  COGS = $182K  |  A/R = $50K  |  Inventory = $30K  |  A/P = $25K
Step 1 — DSO: ($50K / $365K) × 365 = 50 days
Step 2 — DIO: ($30K / $182K) × 365 = 60 days
Step 3 — DPO: ($25K / $182K) × 365 = 50 days
Step 4 — CCC: DSO + DIO - DPO = 50 + 60 - 50 = 60 days
Step 5 — Ratios (assume CA = $100K, CL = $60K): Current Ratio = $100K / $60K = 1.67  |  Quick Ratio = ($100K - $30K) / $60K = 1.17
CCC = 60 days  |  Current Ratio = 1.67  |  Quick Ratio = 1.17  |  DSO = 50d  |  DIO = 60d  |  DPO = 50d
Negative CCC (DPO > DSO + DIO) means you're getting paid BEFORE you have to pay suppliers — the best possible cash position. Amazon famously ran negative CCC for years, effectively using supplier float to fund operations.

15. SaaS Metrics — ARR, Churn, CAC/LTV

These are the scorecard numbers for any subscription business. Investors obsess over them because they're the leading indicators of whether the business model actually works or is just burning cash to stand still. Rule: LTV should be at least 3x CAC. CAC payback should be under 12 months. Monthly churn above 5% is essentially a death spiral — you lose 60% of customers in a year.
ARR = MRR × 12     MRR = Customers × ARPU
Gross Churn Rate = (Lost Customers / Starting Customers) × 100%
Net Revenue Churn = ((MRR Lost − MRR Expansion) / Starting MRR) × 100%
CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost) = Total Sales & Marketing Spend / New Customers Acquired
LTV = ARPU × Gross Margin % / Churn Rate     OR    ARPU × Average Customer Lifespan
LTV:CAC = LTV / CAC     >3:1 healthy  |  <1:1 means you're burning more than you ever recover
CAC Payback (months) = CAC / (ARPU × Gross Margin)     Target: <12 months
Net Promoter Score (NPS) = % Promoters − % Detractors
Magic Number = Net New MRR / Previous Quarter's Sales & Marketing Spend     >1.0 is efficient
SaaS Metrics — Full Worked Example
Monthly Revenue = $120K  |  200 customers  |  ARPU = $600/mo  |  Gross Margin = 75%
New customers this month = 25  |  Sales & Marketing spend = $30K  |  Churned MRR = $4K  |  Expansion MRR = $2K
Previous Q S&M spend = $80K  |  Net new MRR this Q = $15K
ARR: $120K × 12 = $1,440,000
CAC: $30K / 25 customers = $1,200 per customer
LTV (gross margin method): $600 × 75% / (4K/120K) = $450 / 0.0333 = $13,500
LTV:CAC: $13,500 / $1,200 = 11.25x  (excellent — 11.25x return per customer)
Net Revenue Churn: ($4K − $2K) / $120K = $2K / $120K = 1.67% monthly
CAC Payback: $1,200 / ($600 × 75%) = $1,200 / $450 = 2.67 months
Magic Number: $15K net new MRR / $80K S&M = 0.19  (needs improvement — aim for >1.0)
ARR = $1.44M  |  CAC = $1,200  |  LTV = $13,500  |  LTV:CAC = 11.25x  |  Net Churn = 1.67%  |  Payback = 2.67 mo  |  Magic # = 0.19
Negative net churn (expansion MRR > churned MRR) means you're growing even when no new customers sign up — the holy grail of SaaS. Zscaler, Twilio, and Snowflake all achieved this. Positive net churn means you're on a treadmill, replacing every lost customer just to stand still.
Monthly churn kill curve: 5% monthly churn = 54% annual retention (you lose half your customers in a year). 2% monthly churn = 79% annual retention. 1% monthly churn = 89% annual retention. The difference between 2% and 1% sounds small but is worth millions at scale.
