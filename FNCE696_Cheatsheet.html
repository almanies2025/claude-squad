Real Talk: Pre-money valuation is what the company is worth before the investor writes a check. Post-money is what it's worth after — the pre-money plus the fresh capital. The VC Method works backwards from an ambitious exit to tell you what the company is worth today. The trap: forgetting that the option pool lives inside the pre-money, which dilutes founders more than VCs expect.
POST-MONEY = PRE-MONEY + Investment
PRE-MONEY = POST-MONEY − Investment
VC Method: Pre-money = Exit Value / (1 + Target IRR)n
Ownership % = Investment / Post-money
Worked Example — Post-Money, Pre-Money, VC Method
Given: Angel invests $500K for 20% equity | Target exit: $20M in Year 5 | Target IRR: 40%
Step 1 — Find Post-money: Post-money = Investment / Ownership % = $500,000 / 20% = $2,500,000
Why: Paying 20% for $500K means the whole company is worth $2.5M after the money arrives.
Step 2 — Find Pre-money: Pre-money = Post-money − Investment = $2,500,000 − $500,000 = $2,000,000
Why: The $500K is new money — the founders owned $2M worth of the pie before it arrived.
Step 3 — VC Method (work backwards from exit): PV = $20,000,000 / (1.40)5 = $20M / 5.3783 = $3,719,000
Why: At 40% annual returns, $3.72M today grows to $20M in 5 years. This is what the 20% stake costs in today's money.
Step 4 — Check implied ownership at VC Method price: $500K / $3.72M = 13.4% — not 20%!
Why: The VC Method produces a higher valuation than the $500K deal implies. The angel's money buys 20% of the $2.5M post-money, but at a discount to the intrinsic VC Method value.
Post-money = $2,500,000 | Pre-money = $2,000,000 | VC Method implied pre-money = $3,719,000 | Angel ownership = 20%
Exam Trick: When you see "option pool" — ask: is it pre-money or post-money? YC and most VCs require the option pool inside pre-money, meaning founders absorb that dilution, not the incoming investor.
Real Talk: A SAFE is Y Combinator's lightweight alternative to a convertible note — it is not debt, has no interest rate, no maturity date, and no repayment obligation. The founder wins because there's no countdown clock. The investor wins because they get either a discount or a valuation cap — whichever gives them more shares. The math contest: cap beats discount when the round is at a high enough valuation that the cap-price is below the discounted round price.
SAFE conversion price = MIN(Cap / Pre-money shares, Round price × (1 − Discount))
Shares received = SAFE amount / Conversion price
Discount price = Round price × (1 − d) | Cap price = Cap / Pre-money shares outstanding
Worked Example — SAFE: Cap vs Discount, Which Wins?
Given: SAFE amount = $200,000 | Valuation cap = $3,000,000 | Discount = 20% | Series A pre-money = $8,000,000 | Existing shares = 4,000,000 (founders) | New option pool = 1,000,000
Step 1 — Find the round price per share: Total shares = 4M (founders) + 1M (option pool) = 5M shares | Round price = $8,000,000 / 5,000,000 = $1.60/share
Why: The option pool is carved out of pre-money, so it increases share count but not the dollar pre-money.
Step 2 — Path A: Calculate the Discount price: Discount price = Round price × (1 − Discount) = $1.60 × 0.80 = $1.28/share
Why: The SAFE investor gets to pretend they bought at 20% below the round price.
Step 2 — Path B: Calculate the Cap price: Cap price = Cap / Pre-money shares = $3,000,000 / 4,000,000 = $0.75/share
Why: The $3M cap on 4M pre-money shares means the SAFE investor gets to pretend they bought at $0.75/share — way below the $1.60 Series A price.
Step 3 — Pick the LOWER price (better for the SAFE investor): MIN($1.28, $0.75) = $0.75/share — the Cap wins
Why: The SAFE investor always takes whichever price gives them more shares. Here the cap decisively beats the discount.
Step 4 — Compute SAFE shares received: Shares = SAFE amount / Conversion price = $200,000 / $0.75 = 266,667 shares
Step 5 — Find SAFE ownership %: Total shares after = 5,000,000 + 266,667 = 5,266,667 | SAFE ownership = 266,667 / 5,266,667 = 5.06%
Why: If the discount had won instead, shares = $200K / $1.28 = 156,250 = 2.97%. The cap earns 2.1 extra percentage points of ownership.
SAFE conversion price = $0.75/share (cap wins) | SAFE shares = 266,667 | SAFE ownership = 5.06%
Key Insight: The SAFE cap references pre-money shares (excluding the new option pool). A $3M cap on 4M founder shares = $0.75/share equivalent. Series A investors pay $1.60/share. The SAFE investor saves $0.85/share — this is the economic benefit of the cap.
Common Mistake: Students sometimes divide the cap by post-option-pool shares — wrong! SAFE caps always reference pre-money shares at the time of conversion, because the option pool hasn't been issued yet from the SAFE's perspective.
Real Talk: Convertible notes are actual debt — they accrue interest and have a maturity date when they must be repaid (or converted). Unlike SAFEs, the interest compounds the principal, so the investor converts more shares at the next round. The cap and discount stack: the note holder gets whichever produces more shares — the lower of (cap-hit price) vs (discounted round price). The classic founder mistake: ignoring the interest accrual, which silently inflates the conversion amount.
Accrued interest = Principal × Rate × Years
Total conversion amount = Principal + Accrued interest
Conversion price = MIN(Cap / Pre-money shares, Round price × (1 − Discount))
Shares received = Total conversion amount / Conversion price
Worked Example — Convertible Note: Interest + Cap + Discount
Given: Principal = $500,000 | Coupon rate = 8% per year | Holding period = 2 years | Valuation cap = $4,000,000 | Discount = 20% | Series B pre-money = $15,000,000 | Pre-money shares = 2,000,000
Step 1 — Compute accrued interest: Accrued interest = Principal × Rate × Years = $500,000 × 8% × 2 = $80,000
Why: Interest is the cost of delaying equity conversion. The VC round is 2 years away, so the investor is "owed" $80K on top of principal.
Step 2 — Compute total conversion amount: Total = $500,000 + $80,000 = $580,000
Why: The note investor converts $580K, not $500K — the interest buys extra shares.
Step 3 — Compute round price per share: Round price = Pre-money / Pre-money shares = $15,000,000 / 2,000,000 = $7.50/share
Step 4 — Path A: Discount price: Discount price = Round price × (1 − Discount) = $7.50 × 0.80 = $6.00/share
Step 4 — Path B: Cap price: Cap price = Cap / Pre-money shares = $4,000,000 / 2,000,000 = $2.00/share
Why: The cap of $4M on 2M shares means $2.00/share equivalent — the investor gets in at a 73% discount to the $7.50 Series B price!
Step 5 — Pick the LOWER price (more shares): MIN($6.00, $2.00) = $2.00/share — Cap wins decisively
Why: When a startup does very well (Series B at $15M pre), the cap saves the note investor from paying full price. They convert at $2 instead of $6.
Step 6 — Compute shares received: Shares = $580,000 / $2.00 = 290,000 shares
Step 7 — Effective discount vs Series B investors: Series B investors pay $7.50/share. Note investor effectively pays: $580K / 290K = $2.00/share. Savings = $5.50/share × 290K shares = $1,595,000 in foregone investment
Accrued interest = $80,000 | Total conversion = $580,000 | Conversion price = $2.00/share | Shares = 290,000 | Effective discount vs Series B = 73.3%
Compare to SAFE: A $500K SAFE with the same $4M cap and 20% discount — no interest accrual, so SAFE converts $500K (not $580K). The note holder gets 290K shares vs SAFE holder's 250K shares. The 2 years of interest bought 40,000 extra shares.
Exam Trap: If you forget to add accrued interest to principal before computing shares, you'll undercount the note holder's shares. Always compound-first (add interest), then convert. Interest is typically simple annual (not compound) unless stated otherwise.
Real Talk: The option pool shuffle is the quietest dilution trap in startup fundraising. When VCs require "10% post-money option pool," they mean: AFTER the new investment, 10% of total shares must be reserved for employees. The shuffle is WHO absorbs those shares — and the answer is always founders, not the VC. The option pool comes out of the pre-money pie, which means the pre-money gets divided into more slices (including the new pool), making each founder slice worth less per dollar of pre-money.
Post-money = Pre-money + Investment
Option pool (post-money %) = New options / Total shares (including new options + new investor shares)
Founder ownership = Founder shares / Total fully-diluted shares
VC ownership = Investment / Post-money valuation
Worked Example — Option Pool Shuffle: Who Really Pays?
Given: Founders have 4,000,000 shares | VC invests $2M at $8M pre-money | VC requires 10% post-money option pool
Step 1 — Find the round price per share (before the pool question): Pre-money = $8,000,000 | Existing pre-money shares = 4,000,000 | Price = $8M / 4M = $2.00/share
Step 2 — CASE A: Option pool carved from PRE-money (founder absorbs): Let new option shares = x. Total post-money shares = 4M (founders) + x (options). We need x / (4M + x) = 10%. So x = 0.10 × (4M + x). Solving: 0.9x = 400,000 → x = 444,444 option shares
Why: The pool comes out of the pre-money pie, so the dilution falls entirely on founders. Founders effectively "give up" 444,444 shares to create the pool.
Step 3 — CASE A: New investor shares: VC investment = $2M at $2.00/share = 1,000,000 shares
Step 4 — CASE A: Final cap table: Total shares = 4M + 444K + 1M = 5,444,444. Founder: 4M/5.44M = 73.5% | Options: 444K/5.44M = 8.2% | VC: 1M/5.44M = 18.4%
Step 5 — CASE B: Option pool carved from POST-money (VC absorbs — same deal terms): Post-money = $8M + $2M = $10M. Options = 10% × $10M = $1M equivalent. In shares: $1M / $2.00 = 500,000 option shares
Step 6 — CASE B: Final cap table: Total shares = 4M + 500K + 1M = 5,500,000. Founder: 4M/5.5M = 72.7% | Options: 500K/5.5M = 9.1% | VC: 1M/5.5M = 18.2%
Step 7 — Compare: Pre-money pool: founder keeps 73.5%. Post-money pool: founder keeps 72.7%. Difference = 0.8% of the company, worth $64,000 at this valuation.
Pre-money pool: founder = 73.5%, VC = 18.4% | Post-money pool: founder = 72.7%, VC = 18.2% | Founder loses 0.8% more with pre-money pool
Real Talk: VCs always negotiate for a post-money pool because it comes out of the pre-money (from founders). Founders should push back: "We'll agree to 10% post-money if you increase the pre-money to compensate us for the dilution." The VC's response tells you everything about their alignment.
Key Number: Pre-money option pool = more founder dilution. Post-money option pool = same dilution to pre-money value but slightly less founder dilution in practice. Always clarify: pre-money or post-money? This one question can be worth 1-2% of the company on a $10M raise.
Real Talk: In a liquidation event (M&A, bankruptcy, forced sale), everyone gets paid in a specific order. The waterfall determines how much each class of equity receives before the next class gets anything. Understanding the waterfall explains why a founder with 60% ownership might walk away with $0 in a bad M&A deal. The two key questions are: (1) Does each preferred class take its preference or convert to common? (2) Does it participate in the remainder after taking preference?
Waterfall order: Secured Debt → Unsecured Debt → Preferred Stock (liquidation pref) → Common Stock
1x Non-participating: MAX(preference amount, converted common value) — picks whichever is bigger
Participating preferred: receives preference AND participates in remainder (double-dip)
Proceeds to common = Total proceeds − All preferred preferences (that chose not to convert)
Worked Example — Waterfall: Who Gets What in an M&A?
Given: Exit EV = $50M | Net Debt = $5M | Available for equity = $45M
Series A: $5M at 1x non-participating preferred | Series B: $10M at 2x participating preferred
Founder common: 8,000,000 shares | Employee options: 2,000,000 shares | Total fully-diluted = 10,000,000
Step 1 — Calculate Series A liquidation preference: Series A pref = $5M × 1x = $5M
Step 2 — Decide: should Series A convert to common? If Series A converts to common: 20% × $45M = $9M. Compare: $9M (convert) vs $5M (pref). MAX($5M, $9M) = $9M — Series A converts to common
Why: Non-participating preferred always compares the preference amount to the converted value and picks the bigger number. Here, converting to common is worth $4M more.
Step 3 — Calculate Series B participating preference: Series B pref = $10M × 2x = $20M (they get this first, before anyone else)
Step 4 — Calculate Series B participation in remainder: Remaining after Series A conversion = $45M (since Series A took $9M as common). Series B participation = 40% × ($45M − $9M) = 40% × $36M = $14.4M
Step 5 — Calculate Series B total proceeds: Series B total = $20M (preference) + $14.4M (participation) = $34.4M
Why: Series B is participating preferred — they get their 2x preference first AND then 40% of what's left after Series A converted to common.
Step 6 — Calculate what's left for common pool: Common pool = $45M (total) − $9M (Series A as common) − $14.4M (Series B participation) = $21.6M
Step 7 — Calculate per-share price for common: Per-share = $21.6M / 10,000,000 shares = $2.16/share
Series A: $9M (converted to common) | Series B: $34.4M total | Common pool: $21.6M | Per share: $2.16
Cap Table Tip: Build the waterfall from the top down. Each class's decision (convert to common or take preference) affects everyone below. Non-participating preferred is "rich enough" to convert when the upside exceeds the preference. Participating preferred ALWAYS converts if there's any upside, because they get both the preference AND a share of the remainder.
The Trap: A 2x participating preferred can receive MORE than 2x their investment in a large exit. Example: $10M invested, $100M exit, 50% participating → $10M (pref) + $45M (50% of remainder) = $55M total — that's 5.5x on a 2x instrument! Always model the waterfall explicitly.
Real Talk: Cap table math is the backbone of every founder/investor negotiation. Every new round, option grant, or convertible instrument changes the ownership pie. The key insight: percentage ownership is only meaningful when you know the fully-diluted denominator. A "60% founder stake" sounds great until you realize there's a hidden option pool that hasn't been issued yet.
Ownership % = Your Shares / Fully-Diluted Shares (founders + investors + options + converts)
New ownership % (after round) = Old ownership % × (Pre-money / Post-money)
Dilution from new options = New options / (Old shares + New options)
Post-money ownership = Investment / Post-money valuation
Worked Example — Cap Table Evolution: Seed to Series A
Given: Founders: 6,000,000 shares (post-vesting) | Option pool: 1,000,000 (10%) | Seed investor: $1M at $4M pre-money
Step 1 — Find pre-money shares: Pre-money shares = 6,000,000 (founders) + 1,000,000 (option pool) = 7,000,000 shares
Step 2 — Find pre-money valuation: $4,000,000 (given)
Step 3 — Find price per share: Price = Pre-money / Pre-money shares = $4,000,000 / 7,000,000 = $0.5714/share
Step 4 — Compute seed investor shares: Shares = Investment / Price = $1,000,000 / $0.5714 = 1,750,000 shares
Step 5 — Find post-money valuation: Post-money = Pre-money + Investment = $4M + $1M = $5,000,000
Step 6 — Find post-money shares: Post-money shares = 7,000,000 + 1,750,000 = 8,750,000 shares
Step 7 — Final ownership percentages: Founder: 6M/8.75M = 68.6% | Options: 1M/8.75M = 11.4% | Seed: 1.75M/8.75M = 20.0%
Founder: 68.6% | Option pool: 11.4% | Seed investor: 20.0% | Price per share: $0.5714
Worked Example — Down Round: Weighted Average Anti-Dilution
Given: Old price = $2.00 | Old shares = 5,000,000 | New price = $1.00 | New shares = 3,000,000
Step 1 — Apply broad-based weighted average formula: Adjusted price = (Old price × Old shares + New price × New shares) / (Old shares + New shares) = ($2.00 × 5M + $1.00 × 3M) / (5M + 3M) = ($10M + $3M) / 8M = $1.625/share
Why: The weighted average "splits the difference" between the old high price and the new low price, weighted by how many shares each represents.
Step 2 — Compute equivalent new shares for old investors: Old investors receive: 5,000,000 shares × ($1.625 − $1.00) / $1.00 = 5M × $0.625 / $1.00 = 3,125,000 equivalent new shares worth $3.125M at the new price
Step 3 — Verify: Without anti-dilution: old investors have $10M locked in at $2.00. With adjustment at $1.625: 5M shares × $1.625 = $8.125M, still below original $10M. Anti-dilution partially but not fully makes them whole.
Adjusted exercise price = $1.625/share | Old investors made whole on $0.625/share of dilution
Trap: Option pool expansion at a new round = additional dilution to existing holders. If you expand the option pool from 10% to 15% at Series A, the 5% difference comes out of founders' and earlier investors' pockets, not the new investor's.
Real Talk: Vesting aligns founder/employee incentives with the company's long-term survival. The cliff exists so nobody can quit in month 2 and walk away with 16.7% of the company. After the cliff, equity accumulates monthly — you earn it a little bit every day you're still there. Think of vesting as paying for work you've already done: you earned those shares by staying, and if you leave early, the company buys them back at what you paid.
Monthly vesting rate (after cliff) = (100% − Cliff %) / (Total Months − Cliff Month)
Vested % after cliff = Cliff % + (Months after cliff × Monthly rate)
Before cliff = 0% (you forfeit everything if you leave early)
Worked Example — Vesting: Founder Leaves at Month 25
Given: 4-year total (48 months) | 14-month cliff | 20% vests at cliff | monthly thereafter
Step 1 — Cliff check: Month 25 > Month 14 → cliff has been crossed — the 20% cliff shares are vested
Why: The cliff is a one-time gate. Once you've been there long enough to cross it, you keep the cliff shares permanently.
Step 2 — Compute monthly vesting rate after cliff: Remaining months = 48 − 14 = 34 months | Monthly rate = 20% / 34 months = 0.5882% per month
Why: After the cliff, the remaining 80% of the grant vests evenly over the remaining 34 months.
Step 3 — Count months after cliff: Months after cliff = 25 − 14 = 11 months
Step 4 — Compute post-cliff vesting: Post-cliff vesting = 11 × 0.5882% = 6.47%
Step 5 — Total vested: Total = 20.00% (cliff) + 6.47% (post-cliff) = 26.47%
Why: Confirms: 20% + 11 × (20%/34) = 20% + 6.47% = 26.47%.
Vested at Month 25 = 26.47% of the total grant
Worked Example — Vesting: Founder Leaves BEFORE Cliff
Given: Same vesting schedule | Founder leaves at Month 6
Step 1 — Cliff check: Month 6 < Month 14 → cliff NOT yet reached
Step 2 — Check if any vesting occurred: No monthly vesting before the cliff. Monthly vesting only begins after the cliff month.
Vested at Month 6 = 0% — founder forfeits all equity
Rule of thumb: The monthly rate is calculated on the REMAINING pool after the cliff: (100% − 20%) / (48 − 14) = 2.353% per month of the remaining grant. Some texts express this as a flat monthly % of the original grant (20%/48 = 0.4167%), which yields different answers — use the post-cliff method above for cliffed schedules.
Real Talk: Beyond basic vesting, real option math involves early exercise, disqualified dispositions, and the choice between ISO vs NSO taxation. The difference can be worth hundreds of thousands of dollars in taxes. ISO gives you favorable capital gains treatment if you hold long enough, but has AMT (Alternative Minimum Tax) risk. NSO is simpler but taxes the spread as ordinary income when you exercise.
Intrinsic value = (Market price − Exercise price) × Number of options
Taxable spread (NSO/ISO AMT): FMV at exercise − Exercise price
ISO qualifying: held 2 years from grant + 1 year from exercise → all long-term capital gains
ISO disqualifying: sold before holding periods met → ordinary income on the spread
83(b) election: elect within 30 days of grant → taxed at grant date FMV, not future appreciation
Worked Example — ISO vs NSO: Which Is Better?
Given: 100,000 options granted at $1.00/share | FMV at exercise: $10.00/share | Sale price: $15.00/share
Step 1 — NSO: Tax at exercise (ordinary income): Spread = ($10.00 − $1.00) × 100,000 = $900,000 ordinary income (taxed at up to 37%)
Why: NSOs are taxed as compensation when you exercise. The $9/share spread is treated as wages.
Step 2 — NSO: Tax at sale (capital gains): Gain = ($15.00 − $10.00) × 100,000 = $500,000 capital gain (held >1 year = long-term capital gains at 20%)
Step 3 — ISO qualifying disposition (holding periods met): Entire gain = ($15.00 − $1.00) × 100,000 = $1,400,000 all long-term capital gains
Why: If you hold ISOs long enough (2 years from grant, 1 year from exercise), the entire appreciation is taxed at capital gains rates, not ordinary income rates.
Step 4 — ISO disqualifying disposition (sold early): Spread at exercise = $900K ordinary income + remaining $400K as capital gain
Step 5 — Tax comparison (37% OI rate, 20% LTCG rate): NSO total tax: ($900K × 37%) + ($500K × 20%) = $333K + $100K = $433K. ISO qualifying: $1,400K × 20% = $280K. ISO saves ~$153K
ISO qualifying: $1.4M all LTCG, tax = $280K | NSO: $900K OI + $500K LTCG, tax = $433K | ISO advantage: ~$153K
Worked Example — 83(b) Election: Lock In Low Tax Now
Given: 1,000,000 options | Grant price: $0.001/share | FMV at grant: $0.10/share | 83(b) election filed on day 1
Step 1 — Compute taxable amount under 83(b): Taxable at grant = (FMV − Exercise price) × Shares = ($0.10 − $0.001) × 1,000,000 = $99,000 ordinary income
Why: 83(b) lets you pay tax NOW at today's low valuation instead of later at a potentially much higher valuation.
Step 2 — Compare: without 83(b), at exit (FMV = $1.00/share): Without 83(b): ($1.00 − $0.001) × 1M = $999K ordinary income at exercise. With 83(b): already paid tax on $99K, the remaining $900K of appreciation is taxed as capital gains.
Step 3 — Tax savings: Without 83(b): $999K × 37% = $369.6K. With 83(b): $99K × 37% + $900K × 20% = $36.6K + $180K = $216.6K. 83(b) saves ~$153K
83(b) election locks in low ordinary income tax now vs potentially much higher tax later
Rule of thumb: File 83(b) when the spread is small (startup at low valuation) or when you expect the FMV to appreciate dramatically before you can exercise. The 30-day window is absolutely unforgiving — missed deadline = no second chance.
AMT trap for ISOs: The spread on ISO exercise is an AMT preference item. If you exercise ISOs in a year where the spread is large (company doing well = high FMV), you may owe AMT even if you haven't sold the shares. Model this before large early exercises.
Real Talk: Net Working Capital answers: "If we collected everything we're owed and paid everyone we owe tomorrow, how much cash would we have left?" Current Ratio asks: "Do we have enough total current assets to cover all our current liabilities?" Quick Ratio strips inventory because you can't always sell it fast enough to pay a bill tomorrow.
NWC = (Cash + A/R + Inventory) − (A/P + Accrued Expenses + Current Tax Payable)
Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
Quick Ratio = (Cash + A/R) / Current Liabilities
Cash Ratio = Cash / Current Liabilities (most conservative — cash only)
Worked Example — Liquidity Ratios
Given: Cash = $50K | A/R = $80K | Inventory = $40K | A/P = $60K | Accrued Expenses = $30K | Current Tax = $50K
Step 1 — Calculate Current Assets: CA = Cash + A/R + Inventory = $50K + $80K + $40K = $170K
Step 2 — Calculate Current Liabilities: CL = A/P + Accrued Expenses + Current Tax = $60K + $30K + $50K = $140K
Step 3 — Calculate NWC: NWC = $170K − $140K = $30K
Why: Positive NWC means you have $30K of liquid cushion after covering all short-term obligations.
Step 4 — Calculate Current Ratio: $170K / $140K = 1.21x
Why: Above 1.0 means technically solvent on a short-term basis. Below 1.0 means you're technically insolvent.
Step 5 — Calculate Quick Ratio: ($50K + $80K) / $140K = $130K / $140K = 0.93x
Why: Below 1.0 means inventory is doing the heavy lifting. If you had to pay all liabilities tomorrow, you'd be $10K short without selling inventory.
Step 6 — Calculate Cash Ratio: $50K / $140K = 0.36x
NWC = $30K | Current Ratio = 1.21x | Quick Ratio = 0.93x | Cash Ratio = 0.36x
Real Talk: The Cash Conversion Cycle answers: "How many days does it take from paying for raw materials to collecting from customers?" Fewer days = healthier cash flow. If your CCC is 60 days and a competitor runs it in 30, they're effectively getting a 30-day free loan on every dollar of working capital. Negative CCC means you're getting paid before you have to pay suppliers — the holy grail of cash management.
DSO = (A/R / Sales) × 365 — how many days to collect from customers
DIO = (Inventory / COGS) × 365 — how many days inventory sits unsold
DPO = (A/P / COGS) × 365 — how many days before you pay suppliers
CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO — net days your cash is tied up
Worked Example — Cash Conversion Cycle
Given: A/R = $80K | Inventory = $40K | A/P = $60K | Sales = $500K | COGS = $300K
Step 1 — Calculate DSO (Days Sales Outstanding): DSO = ($80K / $500K) × 365 = 0.16 × 365 = 58.4 days
Why: On average, it takes 58 days to collect payment from customers. The longer this number, the more working capital you need to fund operations.
Step 2 — Calculate DIO (Days Inventory Outstanding): DIO = ($40K / $300K) × 365 = 0.1333 × 365 = 48.7 days
Why: Inventory sits for about 49 days before it sells. High DIO means capital is tied up in goods that aren't yet revenue.
Step 3 — Calculate DPO (Days Payable Outstanding): DPO = ($60K / $300K) × 365 = 0.20 × 365 = 73.0 days
Why: We take 73 days to pay suppliers. The longer this, the more we can use supplier money to fund operations.
Step 4 — Calculate Cash Conversion Cycle: CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO = 58.4 + 48.7 − 73.0 = 34.1 days
Why: We pay suppliers in 73 days but collect from customers in 107 days. We need working capital to bridge the 34-day gap.
DSO = 58.4d | DIO = 48.7d | DPO = 73.0d | CCC = 34.1 days
DPO > DSO is ideal — you're stretching out what you owe while collecting faster than you restock. Amazon famously ran negative CCC for years, effectively using supplier float to fund operations. Negative CCC means customers pay before suppliers need to be paid.
Real Talk: EPS tells you how much profit each share of common stock "owns." Diluted EPS goes further and assumes every option, warrant, and convertible security has been exercised — because in a real exit, they all convert and dilute common shareholders. The Treasury Stock Method is a clever accounting trick: it pretends the company uses the money it would receive from option exercises to buy back shares at the market price, then adds only the net new shares to the denominator.
Basic EPS = (Net Income − Preferred Dividends) / Weighted Average Common Shares Outstanding
Diluted EPS = (Net Income − Preferred Dividends) / (Weighted Avg Shares + Net New Shares from TS Method)
Treasury Stock Method: Net new shares = Options Exercised − (Proceeds / Market Price)
Worked Example — EPS: Basic vs Diluted
Given: Net Income = $2M | Preferred Dividends = $200K | Avg Common Shares = 800,000 | In-the-money options: 100,000 shares | Avg exercise price = $20 | Avg market price = $50
Step 1 — Calculate Basic EPS: (Net Income − Pref Div) / Avg Common = ($2M − $200K) / 800K = $1.8M / 800K = $2.25 per share
Step 2 — Treasury Stock Method — proceeds from exercise: Proceeds = 100,000 options × $20 exercise price = $2,000,000
Step 3 — Treasury Stock Method — shares repurchased: Shares repurchased = $2,000,000 / $50 market price = 40,000 shares
Why: The company would use the $2M proceeds to buy 40K shares at $50 each. Those 40K shares are effectively "cancelled out" against the 100K options.
Step 4 — Treasury Stock Method — net dilutive shares: Net new shares = 100,000 − 40,000 = 60,000 net new shares
Step 5 — Calculate Diluted EPS: $1.8M / (800K + 60K) = $1.8M / 860K = $2.09 per share
Step 6 — Calculate dilution percentage: (Basic − Diluted) / Basic = ($2.25 − $2.09) / $2.25 = 7.1% dilution
Basic EPS = $2.25 | Diluted EPS = $2.09 | Dilution = 7.1%
Worked Example — Dilutive Convertible Notes
Given: $5M convertible note, converts to 200,000 shares at $25/share | Market price at conversion = $40/share
Step 1 — If-converted method: Assume conversion at issuance → adds 200,000 shares to denominator immediately. Dilutive shares = 200,000 shares
Step 2 — Treasury stock method check: Net dilutive shares = 200K × ($40 − $25) / $40 = 200K × 0.375 = 75,000 net new shares
Why: The convertible note holder converts at $25 but the shares are worth $40 — they get $15/share of "bonus" value per share, which in TS method terms is treated as if the company repurchased shares with the spread.
Step 3 — Rule: Use whichever method gives HIGHER EPS (less dilution) for standard reporting — but for full dilution use if-converted method. Here, TS method gives fewer dilutive shares (75K vs 200K), so standard diluted EPS would use TS method.
If-converted: 200K dilutive shares | TS method: 75K dilutive shares
Anti-dilutive trap: Out-of-the-money options (exercise price > average market price) add ZERO dilution. Always check: is the option in or out of the money before calculating? If out-of-the-money, exclude from the calculation entirely.
Real Talk: These are the scorecard numbers for any subscription business. Investors obsess over them because they're leading indicators of whether the business model actually works or is just burning cash. Rule of thumb: LTV should be at least 3x CAC. CAC payback should be under 12 months. Monthly churn above 5% is essentially a death spiral — you lose 60% of customers in a year.
ARR = MRR × 12
Net Revenue Churn = (MRR Lost − MRR Expansion) / Starting MRR
CAC = Total Sales & Marketing Spend / New Customers Acquired
LTV = ARPU × Gross Margin % / Churn Rate
LTV:CAC = LTV / CAC (>3:1 healthy, <1:1 means burning more than you recover)
CAC Payback (months) = CAC / (ARPU × Gross Margin) (target <12 months)
Magic Number = Net New MRR / Previous Quarter's Sales & Marketing Spend (>1.0 efficient)
Worked Example — SaaS Metrics
Given: Monthly Revenue = $120K | 200 customers | ARPU = $600/mo | Gross Margin = 75% | New customers = 25 | S&M spend = $30K | Churned MRR = $4K | Expansion MRR = $2K | Previous Q S&M = $80K | Net new MRR this Q = $15K
Step 1 — Calculate ARR: $120K × 12 = $1,440,000
Step 2 — Calculate CAC: $30K / 25 customers = $1,200 per customer
Step 3 — Calculate Churn Rate: Churn rate = Churned MRR / Starting MRR = $4K / $120K = 3.33% monthly churn
Step 4 — Calculate LTV (gross margin method): LTV = ($600 × 75%) / 0.0333 = $450 / 0.0333 = $13,500
Why: Each customer generates $450 gross profit per month, and at 3.33% monthly churn, the average customer lasts about 30 months. $450 × 30 = $13,500 lifetime gross profit.
Step 5 — Calculate LTV:CAC ratio: $13,500 / $1,200 = 11.25x
Why: For every $1 spent acquiring a customer, you get $11.50 back over the customer's lifetime. Above 3:1 is considered healthy; 11.25x is excellent.
Step 6 — Calculate Net Revenue Churn: ($4K − $2K) / $120K = $2K / $120K = 1.67% monthly
Why: Even though $4K of MRR churned, $2K of expansion offset it. Net churn is only 1.67% — positive but manageable.
Step 7 — Calculate CAC Payback: $1,200 / ($600 × 75%) = $1,200 / $450 = 2.67 months
Why: It takes 2.67 months of gross profit from each customer to pay back the acquisition cost. Target is under 12 months — this company is well within that.
Step 8 — Calculate Magic Number: $15K net new MRR / $80K S&M = 0.19
Why: Magic Number measures sales efficiency. 0.19 means for every dollar of S&M spent last quarter, only $0.19 of new ARR was added this quarter. Target is >1.0 — this company needs to improve sales efficiency.
ARR = $1.44M | CAC = $1,200 | LTV = $13,500 | LTV:CAC = 11.25x | Net Churn = 1.67% | Payback = 2.67 mo | Magic # = 0.19
Negative net churn (expansion MRR > churned MRR) means growing even when no new customers sign up — the holy grail of SaaS. Positive net churn means you're on a treadmill, replacing every lost customer just to stand still.
Monthly churn kill curve: 5% monthly churn = 54% annual retention (you lose half your customers in a year). 2% monthly churn = 79% annual retention. The difference between 2% and 1% sounds small but is worth millions at scale.

Real Talk: In a liquidity event, everyone's payout depends on two things: the exit enterprise value and where you sit in the cap table. The liquidation waterfall (who gets paid in what order) determines whether your equity is worth the paper it's printed on. Always start with the exit EV, subtract net debt, then apply liquidation preferences from the top down.
Equity Value at Exit = Exit EV − Net Debt − Preferred Liquidation Preference
Your Proceeds = Equity Value × Your Ownership %
MOIC = Proceeds / Investment
ROI = (Proceeds / Investment) − 1
IRR solves: Σ(Cash Flow_t / (1+IRR)^t) = 0
Worked Example — M&A Exit Returns
Given: Exit EV = $50M | Net Debt = $10M | Series A: $5M at 1.5x liquidation pref | Series A investment = $5M | Founder common = 40%
Step 1 — Calculate equity value (after net debt): Equity = $50M − $10M = $40M
Step 2 — Series A liquidation preference: $5M × 1.5x = $7.5M (Series A gets paid first)
Step 3 — Remaining after pref: $40M − $7.5M = $32.5M (common equity pool)
Step 4 — Founder proceeds (common): $32.5M × 40% = $13.0M
Step 5 — Series A total proceeds: $7.5M (preference) + $32.5M × 60% = $7.5M + $19.5M = $27.0M
Step 6 — Series A MOIC and IRR: MOIC = $27M / $5M = 5.4x | IRR (over 4 years) ≈ 52.5%
Step 7 — Founder MOIC: Founder invested essentially $0 (founding equity) → proceeds $13M = infinite MOIC
Founder: $13.0M | Series A: $27.0M | Series A ROI: 5.4x / ~52.5% IRR
Worked Example — IPO vs M&A for Common Stock
Given: Company exit = $100M | Preferred = $30M (1.0x non-participating) | Common = 70%
Step 1 — M&A: Liquidation preference triggers: Pref takes $30M (liquidation pref). Remaining = $100M − $30M = $70M goes to common. But pref has priority — pref gets $30M + common pool = $100M total. Common = $0
Why: Non-participating preferred gets either its preference OR converted common value, whichever is bigger. Here $30M > 0, so it takes the $30M and leaves nothing for common.
Step 2 — IPO: No liquidation preference: All equity participates at market prices. Common value = $100M × 70% = $70M
M&A common: $0 | IPO common: $70M — structure matters enormously
