FNCE696 · SMU MBA · Valuation Instruments, Cap Tables & Dilution
Deep Dive Edition · EF Exam 2026
1 · POST-MONEY vs PRE-MONEY VALUATION (VC Method)
Real Talk: Pre-money = what investors think the company is worth BEFORE they put money in. Post-money = pre-money + new investment. Investor ownership % = investment ÷ post-money. Founders own pre-money ÷ post-money. Students always trip up on which denominator to use.
Investor % = Investment ÷ Post-Money  |  Founder % = Pre-Money ÷ Post-Money  |  Post-Money = Pre-Money + Investment
Worked Example — VC Method
Given: Pre-money = $4M  |  Investment = $1M  |  8M founder shares outstanding
Q1 — Founder % and Investor % after this round?
  Step 1: Post-Money = $4M + $1M = $5M
    Intuition: Investor is buying a slice of the pie — add their money to the pie's total value.
  Step 2: Investor % = $1M ÷ $5M = 20%
  Step 3: Founder % = $4M ÷ $5M = 80%

Q2 — Price per share (PPS)? Founder shares = 8M.
  Step 1: Total shares = Founder shares ÷ Founder % = 8M ÷ 80% = 10M shares
    Intuition: If founders own 80% and have 8M shares, 8M must be 80% of total.
  Step 2: PPS = Post-Money ÷ Total shares = $5M ÷ 10M = $0.50/share

Q3 — New investor puts $2M at $2/share. What's pre-money?
  Step 1: New shares issued = $2M ÷ $2 = 1M new shares
  Step 2: Existing shares = 9M shares × $2/share = $18M pre-money
Exam tip: Always calculate post-money FIRST. Then investor % = investment ÷ post-money. Never divide by pre-money.
Warning: Students often flip the formula and divide investment by pre-money. That gives the wrong answer — always use post-money as the denominator for investor %.
2 · SAFE NOTES — Valuation Cap vs Discount
Real Talk: A SAFE is NOT a loan — no interest, no maturity date. It gives investors the right to buy shares at a优惠 price when the Series A closes. The cap sets a CEILING on the price per share. The discount sets a percentage OFF the Series A price. The investor gets whichever price is LOWER (more shares for their money).
SAFE Price = MIN(Cap ÷ Pre-SAFE Shares,   Series A Price × (1 − Discount%))
Worked Example — SAFE Cap vs Discount
Given: SAFE cap = $5M  |  Discount = 20%  |  Series A pre-money = $8M  |  10M pre-SAFE shares
Step 1 — Calculate the cap price per share:
  Cap price = $5M ÷ 10M = $0.50/share
  Intuition: The SAFE investor will never pay more than $0.50/share regardless of Series A pricing.

Step 2 — Calculate the discounted Series A price:
  Series A price = $8M ÷ 10M = $0.80/share
  Discounted price = $0.80 × (1 − 0.20) = $0.80 × 0.80 = $0.64/share
  Intuition: Discount gives 20% off the Series A price — but cap might be lower.

Step 3 — Choose the LOWER price (more shares for investor):
  SAFE Price = MIN($0.50, $0.64) = $0.50/share ← Cap wins
  vs. Series A investor who pays $0.80/share
  → SAFE investor gets 37.5% more shares for the same money

Step 4 — Who wins? Calculate ownership after conversion:
  SAFE investment = $5M ÷ $0.50 = 10M SAFE shares
  Post-SAFE total = 10M + 10M = 20M shares
  SAFE ownership = 10M ÷ 20M = 50% of the company
Exam tip: Always calculate BOTH cap price and discounted price, then pick the LOWER one. The SAFE investor always wins on the lower price. If cap is below discounted price → cap wins. If discount is deeper → discount wins.
Warning: SAFE has no accrued interest and no maturity date — unlike convertible notes. Don't add interest to SAFE calculations.
3 · CONVERTIBLE NOTES — Interest + Cap + Discount
Real Talk: Unlike SAFE, convertible notes ARE loans — they accrue interest and have a maturity date. At conversion, accrued interest is added to principal first, THEN the total is converted at the better of cap price or discounted price. If not converted by maturity, notes must be repaid as debt.
Accrued Value = Principal × (1 + Rate%)^years  |  Conversion Price = MIN(Cap Price, Discount Price)
Worked Example — Convertible Note
Given: $500K note  |  8% p.a.  |  2 years  |  Cap = $4M  |  Discount = 25%  |  Series A pre-money = $6M  |  6M pre-note shares
Step 1 — Grow the principal with compound interest:
  Accrued value = $500K × (1.08)² = $500K × 1.1664 = $583,200
  Intuition: Interest compounds on interest — Year 1: $500K × 1.08 = $540K. Year 2: $540K × 1.08 = $583,200.

Step 2 — Calculate both conversion prices:
  Cap price = $4M ÷ 6M = $0.667/share
  Discounted Series A = ($6M ÷ 6M) × (1 − 0.25) = $1.00 × 0.75 = $0.75/share
  Intuition: Cap price = ceiling on valuation per share. Discount price = Series A price minus discount.

Step 3 — Choose the LOWER price (more shares):
  Conversion price = MIN($0.667, $0.75) = $0.667/share ← Cap wins

Step 4 — How many shares does the note convert to?
  Shares = $583,200 ÷ $0.667 = 874,000 shares
  Intuition: More accrued interest → more shares at conversion.

Step 5 — Note holder ownership %:
  Total post-conversion = 6M + 874K = 6.874M shares
  Ownership % = 874K ÷ 6.874M = 12.7%
Exam tip: Convertible notes vs SAFE: SAFE has NO interest, NO maturity. Notes compound interest first, then convert. Always compound interest BEFORE calculating shares.
Warning: Failing to compound the interest is the #1 mistake on exam questions. Interest accrues on the growing balance — use (1+r)^t, not r×t.
4 · DILUTION — Option Pool & New Round
Real Talk: Dilution = new shares issued reduce everyone's ownership %. The option pool (ESOP) is typically created BEFORE a new investor enters — it comes out of founder shares. Both founders AND the option pool dilute pro-rata when new money arrives. The option pool does NOT shield founders — it's just another holder.
Investor % = New Shares ÷ (Existing + New)  |  Post-Dilution Founder % = Pre × (1 − Dilution%)
Worked Example — Dilution with Option Pool
Given: Pre-round: Founders 8M shs (80%) | ESOP 2M shs (20%) | Total 10M  |  New investor: $3M at $1.50/share → 2M new shares
Pre-round ownership — confirm the baseline:
  Founders: 8M ÷ 10M = 80% | ESOP: 2M ÷ 10M = 20%
  Intuition: The ESOP is already issued — it's a real holder like any other shareholder.

Step 1 — Calculate new total shares after investment:
  New total = 10M + 2M = 12M shares
  Intuition: Adding new shares always increases total — existing holders get diluted.

Step 2 — Investor ownership %:
  Investor % = 2M ÷ 12M = 16.67%

Step 3 — Dilution rate applied to each existing holder:
  Total dilution rate = 2M ÷ 12M = 16.67%
  Founder dilution = 80% × (1 − 0.1667) = 66.67%
  ESOP dilution = 20% × (1 − 0.1667) = 16.67%
  New investor = 16.67%

Verify total: 66.67 + 16.67 + 16.67 = 100% ✓
Exam tip: Option pool dilution — the pool is created BEFORE the new investor. Founders get diluted BY the pool creation too, not just by the new investor. Always start with pre-existing share counts.
Warning: The option pool does NOT protect founders from dilution. Wrong intuition: "ESOP takes hits so founders don't." Correct: everyone dilutes pro-rata. The pool is just another shareholder.
5 · CAP TABLE — Full 4-Round Walkthrough
Real Talk: A cap table tracks ownership % as a company raises across multiple rounds. Pre-money is the company's value before the new round. Price per share = pre-money ÷ pre-round total shares. New shares = investment ÷ PPS. Work round by round — always track cumulative shares.
Worked Example — Full Cap Table
Given: Founders: 8M shs @$0.05  |  Seed: $500K at $0.20/sh  |  Series A: $2M at $0.60/sh  |  Series B: $4M at $1.60/sh
Round 0 — Pre-Seed (Founders own 100%):
  Price per share (PPS) = $0.05 | Total shares = 8M
  Company value = 8M × $0.05 = $400K

After Seed ($500K at $0.20/share):
  New shares = $500K ÷ $0.20 = 2.5M
  Total = 8M + 2.5M = 10.5M
  Founders: 8M÷10.5M = 76.2% | Seed: 2.5M÷10.5M = 23.8%

After Series A ($2M at $0.60/share):
  New shares = $2M ÷ $0.60 = 3.333M
  Total = 10.5M + 3.333M = 13.833M
  Founders: 57.8% | Seed: 18.1% | Series A: 24.1%

After Series B ($4M at $1.60/share):
  New shares = $4M ÷ $1.60 = 2.5M
  Total = 13.833M + 2.5M = 16.333M
  Founders: 49.0% | Seed: 15.3% | Series A: 20.4% | Series B: 15.3%

Verify: 49.0 + 15.3 + 20.4 + 15.3 = 100% ✓
6 · LIQUIDATION PREFERENCE — 1× vs 2× Participating
Real Talk: Liquidation preference = who gets paid first when the company is sold. Non-participating investors choose the better of their preference OR pro-rata share. Participating investors get BOTH — preference first, then their share of the remainder pro-rata. Participating is much more valuable for investors at large exits.
1× Non-Part: Max(Preference, Pro-Rata)  |  Participating: Preference + (Remaining × Ownership%)
Worked Example — Liquidation Preference
Given: Exit = $15M  |  Investor put in $5M  |  Investor owns 30% post-money  |  Compare: 1× Non-Part, 2× Non-Part, 1× Participating
Scenario A — 1× Non-Participating:
  Preference = 1 × $5M = $5M
  Pro-rata = 30% × $15M = $4.5M
  Investor takes Max($5M, $4.5M) = $5M ← preference wins
  Remaining $10M → Founders get 70% = $7M
  Intuition: Investor chose $5M over their 30% pro-rata share of $15M.

Scenario B — 2× Non-Participating:
  Preference = 2 × $5M = $10M
  Remaining = $15M − $10M = $5M
  Founders get remaining $5M = $5M
  Intuition: 2× preference means investor takes $10M first — founders get scraps.

Scenario C — 1× Participating:
  Investor takes $5M (preference) FIRST
  Remaining = $15M − $5M = $10M
  Investor gets 30% × $10M = $3M MORE
  Total investor = $5M + $3M = $8M | Founders = $7M
  Intuition: Participating = preference AND pro-rata. At big exits this dramatically favors investors.
Exam tip: Non-participating investors are smart when the exit is small (preference wins). Participating investors are smart when the exit is large (they get both preference and upside). Know which scenario favors which structure.
Warning: In participating preferred, investors can double-dip — they get their preference AND their pro-rata share of remaining proceeds. This is why participating preferred is the most founder-friendly in negotiations.

7 · IRR — Internal Rate of Return
Real Talk: IRR is the annual return rate that makes NPV of all cash flows = zero. Think of it as: "What interest rate would I need at the bank to get the same result?" Higher IRR = better return. VCs target 25–35% IRR. For single investment + exit with no interim flows: IRR = (MOIC)^(1/Years) − 1.
Simple IRR = (Exit ÷ Investment)^(1÷Years) − 1  |  NPV = Σ CF_t ÷ (1+IRR)^t = 0
Worked Example — IRR (Simple & With Interim Cash Flows)
Given: Invest $2M  |  Exit $10M  |  5-year hold  |  Interim: Yr2 +$1M, Yr5 +$5M
Q1 — Simple IRR (no interim cash flows):
  Step 1: MOIC = $10M ÷ $2M = 5.0×
  Intuition: 5× return on investment over 5 years.
  Step 2: IRR = (5.0)^(1÷5) − 1 = 5.0^0.20 − 1
  Step 3: 5^0.20 = 1.3797 → IRR = 37.97% ≈ 38%

Q2 — With interim cash flows: Yr0: −$2M | Yr2: +$1M | Yr5: +$5M
  Step 1: Try IRR = 20%:
    NPV = −2 + 1/(1.20)² + 5/(1.20)^5
    = −2 + 0.694 + 2.01 = +0.70 (NPV > 0, IRR > 20%)
  Step 2: Try IRR = 30%:
    NPV = −2 + 1/(1.30)² + 5/(1.30)^5
    = −2 + 0.591 + 1.35 = −0.06 (NPV < 0, IRR < 30%)
  Step 3: IRR is between 20% and 30%, closer to 20% → ≈ 28–29%
  Excel: =IRR({-2000000, 0, 1000000, 0, 0, 5000000}) = ~28.6%
Quick estimates for MCQ: 5× in 5 years ≈ 38% IRR. 3× in 3 years ≈ 44% IRR. 2× in 2 years ≈ 41% IRR. These are close enough to eliminate wrong answers fast.
Warning: IRR can be misleading with uneven cash flows — always draw the timeline first. A project with early positive cash flows can have a high IRR but low actual profit.
8 · DCF — Discounted Cash Flow with Terminal Value
Real Talk: DCF values a company by predicting future cash flows and discounting them back to today. Money today is worth more than money tomorrow (opportunity cost). The discount rate = WACC. Terminal value captures everything after the forecast period — usually 70–85% of total DCF value. Get WACC right and get g (growth rate) right.
Enterprise Value = Σ PV(FCF_t) + PV(Terminal Value)  |  TV = FCF_n × (1+g) ÷ (WACC−g)
Worked Example — DCF (3-Year Forecast)
Given: FCF Y1=$100K, Y2=$120K, Y3=$150K  |  WACC=12%  |  g=3%  |  Terminal FCF = Y3 × (1+g)
Step 1 — Calculate Terminal Value (at end of Year 3):
  TV = FCF₃ × (1+g) ÷ (WACC − g)
  TV = $150K × 1.03 ÷ (0.12 − 0.03) = $154.5K ÷ 0.09 = $1,717K
  Intuition: After Year 3 we assume FCF grows forever at g%. The Gordon Growth Model capitalizes that perpetual stream.

Step 2 — Discount each FCF to present value:
  PV(Y1) = $100K ÷ 1.12¹ = $100K ÷ 1.12 = $89.3K
  PV(Y2) = $120K ÷ 1.12² = $120K ÷ 1.2544 = $95.7K
  PV(Y3) = $150K ÷ 1.12³ = $150K ÷ 1.4049 = $106.8K
  Intuition: Discounting "undoes" compounding — later cash flows are worth less today.

Step 3 — Discount Terminal Value back to today:
  PV(TV) = $1,717K ÷ 1.12³ = $1,717K ÷ 1.4049 = $1,222.2K
  Intuition: TV is the biggest component. It's discounted by 3 years of WACC.

Step 4 — Sum all present values:
  EV = 89.3 + 95.7 + 106.8 + 1,222.2 = $1,514K ≈ $1.51M
  Terminal Value = 81% of total value — get g right!
Exam tip: If WACC − g gets very small, terminal value explodes. If WACC = g, the formula breaks (division by zero) — Gordon Growth requires WACC > g. Watch for this trap in exam questions.
Warning: Terminal Value dominates DCF (70–85%). Small changes in g or WACC cause massive changes in EV. Exam questions often test your understanding of this sensitivity.
9 · WACC — Weighted Average Cost of Capital
Real Talk: WACC is the average interest rate a company pays on ALL its capital — both equity and debt. It's the discount rate in DCF. Equity holders demand higher returns than debt holders (more risk), so equity is more expensive. The after-tax cost of debt is lower because interest is tax-deductible.
WACC = (E÷V × Re) + (D÷V × Rd × (1−T))  |  E=Equity, D=Debt, V=E+D, T=Tax rate
Worked Example — WACC
Given: Equity = $8M  |  Debt = $2M  |  Re = 18%  |  Rd = 8%  |  Tax = 25%
Step 1 — Find capital weights:
  Total capital V = $8M + $2M = $10M
  E/V = $8M ÷ $10M = 80% (equity weight)
  D/V = $2M ÷ $10M = 20% (debt weight)
  Intuition: Weight by market value, not book value. Debt % matters because interest is deductible.

Step 2 — Calculate each cost component:
  Equity cost = 80% × 18% = 14.4%
  After-tax debt cost = 20% × 8% × (1 − 0.25)
    = 20% × 8% × 0.75 = 20% × 6% = 1.2%
  Intuition: Tax shield: company saves 25% of interest cost. Without tax: 20% × 8% = 1.6%. With tax: 1.2%.

Step 3 — Sum components:
  WACC = 14.4% + 1.2% = 15.6%
Exam tip: If the company has no debt, WACC = Re (just the cost of equity). If the tax rate is zero, use (1−T) = 1. Always check if tax shield is given before applying it.
Warning: WACC is NOT just the average of Re and Rd. It's weighted by the proportion of each source AND the after-tax cost of debt is used. Simple average = (18% + 8%)/2 = 13% — WRONG.
10 · EVA — Economic Value Added (NOPAT Approach)
Real Talk: EVA answers: "Is the company actually creating wealth for shareholders?" A company can be profitable on paper but still destroy value if it doesn't earn more than the cost of ALL capital (debt + equity). EVA = NOPAT − Capital Charge. Positive EVA = management created value. Negative EVA = they destroyed value despite accounting profits.
EVA = NOPAT − (Capital × WACC)  |  NOPAT = Operating Profit × (1 − Tax Rate)
Worked Example — EVA (NOPAT Approach)
Given: Operating profit = $1,200  |  Capital (debt + equity) = $9,000  |  WACC = 10%  |  Tax rate = 40%
Step 1 — Convert accounting profit to NOPAT (true economic profit):
  NOPAT = Operating Profit × (1 − Tax Rate)
  NOPAT = $1,200 × (1 − 0.40) = $720K
  Intuition: NOPAT = what operating profit would be if there were no interest or tax effects — pure business profitability before financing.

Step 2 — Calculate the capital charge (opportunity cost of all capital):
  Capital charge = Capital × WACC = $9,000 × 10% = $900K
  Intuition: $9M of capital costs $900K/year to finance — this is what investors demand.

Step 3 — Subtract capital charge from NOPAT:
  EVA = $720K − $900K = ($180K)

Interpretation: Negative EVA = management destroyed $180K of shareholder wealth. Despite $1,200K in operating profit, the cost of all capital exceeded that profit. The company would have been better off doing nothing.
Exam tip: Positive NOPAT ≠ positive EVA. A company earning 8% on $9M capital ($720K NOPAT) but paying 10% to finance that capital ($900K capital charge) destroys $180K of wealth. Always compare NOPAT to the capital charge.
Warning: Don't confuse NOPAT with net income. NOPAT = Operating Profit × (1 − Tax). It excludes interest expense. Net income = NOPAT − Interest × (1 − Tax). Use NOPAT for EVA.
11 · CONTRIBUTION MARGIN & SUSTAINABLE GROWTH RATE
Real Talk: Contribution Margin = how much each dollar of sales contributes to covering fixed costs after variable costs are paid. It's the "leftover" after the variable cost of making the product. Sustainable Growth Rate (SGR) = max growth rate using only internal funds (retained earnings) — no new equity or debt.
CM% = (Sales − Variable Costs) ÷ Sales  |  SGR = ROE × Retention Ratio  |  Break-even = Fixed Costs ÷ CM%
Worked Example — Contribution Margin + SGR + Break-even
Given: Cash fixed costs = $60K  |  Variable costs = $70K  |  Sales = $100K  |  Net Income = $20K  |  ROE = 15%
Q1 — Contribution Profit Margin %:
  Step 1: Contribution = Sales − Variable Costs = $100K − $70K = $30K
  Step 2: CM% = $30K ÷ $100K = 30%
  Intuition: For every $1 of sales, $0.30 is available to cover fixed costs and profit.

Q2 — Break-even point in sales dollars:
  Break-even = Fixed Costs ÷ CM% = $60K ÷ 0.30 = $200K in sales
  Intuition: Need $200K in sales to exactly cover all costs (zero profit, zero loss).

Q3 — Sustainable Growth Rate:
  Step 1: Retention ratio = 1 − (Dividends ÷ Net Income)
  Assuming no dividends: = 1 − 0 = 100%
  Step 2: SGR = ROE × Retention Ratio = 15% × 100% = 15%
  Intuition: Company can grow 15% per year without external financing. Growth > 15% requires new equity or debt.
Exam tip: If growth rate > SGR, the company needs external financing. If growth rate < SGR, the company generates free cash flow. SGR assumes constant leverage and payout ratio.
Warning: CM% is not the same as gross margin. Gross margin = (Sales − COGS) ÷ Sales. CM% = (Sales − ALL variable costs including selling, admin) ÷ Sales. Use the right variable costs for the right question.
12 · TERMINAL VALUE & COMPARABLE COMPANY VALUATION
Real Talk: Terminal Value (TV) = value of all cash flows beyond the forecast period, captured in a single number today. Gordon Growth Model assumes cash flows grow forever at rate g. Comparable company valuation uses P/E multiples — find a similar public company's P/E ratio and apply it to the target's EPS.
TV = FCF_n × (1+g) ÷ (WACC−g)  |  Target Value = Target EPS × (Comp Price ÷ Comp EPS)
Worked Example — Terminal Value + Comparable Company
Given: Current net income = $20K  |  Next year FCF = $26K  |  g = 7%  |  Required return = 20%  |  Comp: Price=$20, EPS=$2 (not $3), Net Income=$20K, Shares=10K
Q1 — Terminal Value (Gordon Growth Model):
  Step 1: Confirm Year 1 FCF = $26K (next year's cash flow given)
  Step 2: TV = FCF₁ × (1+g) ÷ (WACC − g)
  TV = $26K × 1.07 ÷ (0.20 − 0.07) = $27.82K ÷ 0.13 = $213,846 ≈ $214K
  Intuition: Gordon Growth values the entire company's perpetual cash flow stream after Year 1. WACC − g = 13% is the "spread" — higher spread = lower TV.

Q2 — Comparable Company Valuation (P/E method):
  Step 1: Comparable P/E = Comp Price ÷ Comp EPS = $20 ÷ $2 = 10×
  Intuition: Investors pay $20 for every $2 of earnings — a 10× multiple.
  Step 2: Target EPS = Net Income ÷ Shares = $20K ÷ 10K = $2.00/share
  Step 3: Target value = Target EPS × Comparable P/E = $2.00 × 10 = $20.00 per share
Exam tip: Two TV formulas exist: (1) TV = FCF_n × (1+g)/(WACC−g) and (2) TV = FCF_n/(WACC−g). Formula (1) is correct when FCF_n is the cash flow at END of Year n (already grown by g). Formula (2) is correct when FCF_n is the cash flow at START of Year n. Check which convention the question uses.
Warning: WACC must be > g for Gordon Growth to work. If WACC ≤ g, the formula gives infinity or negative values — a sign the company is in a "growth bubble."

13 · TERM SHEET CLAUSES — What Each Really Means
Real Talk: A term sheet is the blueprint for a VC deal before lawyers draft legal documents. Each clause has direct financial consequences for founders and investors. Anti-dilution protects investors from bad future valuations. Vesting keeps founders working. Board control determines who runs the company.
Anti-Dilution (Ratchet)Broad-based weighted avg: uses ALL shares (including options) to calculate new price. Full-ratchet: replaces ALL shares at the new low price. Both hurt founders when company raises at a lower valuation — they issue more shares to investors to "make up" the difference.
Liquidation Pref (1×)Investor gets 1× their money back FIRST. Non-participating = choose pref OR pro-rata (whichever is better). Participating = BOTH preference AND pro-rata share of remaining. Participating is most favorable to investors at large exits.
Pay-to-PlayInvestors who skip future rounds lose anti-dilution protection or board seats. Prevents "free riders" who don't participate in new rounds. Keeps cap table clean and signals investor commitment.
Pro-Rata RightsInvestors can buy new shares in future rounds to maintain their %. Founders dislike this — dilutes their ability to lead rounds and signals investor distrust of founders' ability to fundraise.
Board CompositionCommon: 2 founders + 1 investor + 1 independent. Investors push for more seats = more control over company decisions. Uneven board splits matter in deadlocked situations.
Vesting (Cliff)Single-trigger: vests immediately on acquisition. Double-trigger: vests on acquisition AND being fired. Founders strongly prefer double-trigger — their equity is protected if they're pushed out after acquisition.
Cumulative DividendsCumulative = unpaid dividends accrue and must be paid at exit. Non-cumulative = forgotten if not declared. Matters a lot in long-hold investments — cumulative preferred is more valuable to investors.
Exam tip: "What are the implications of [clause] for founders vs investors?" Always show BOTH sides. Founders want flexibility; investors want protection. Know which clause favors which side.
Warning: Participating preferred + cumulative dividends = most investor-friendly structure. Full-ratchet anti-dilution = most founder-hostile. Know the spectrum from most to least founder-friendly.
14 · EXIT MATH — M&A vs IPO vs Secondary
Real Talk: How founders and investors actually get their money out. M&A = trade sale to another company. IPO = going public (180-day lock-up typically). Secondary = selling existing shares before a liquidity event. Earlier liquidity = lower required return because risk is reduced.
IRR = (Exit Proceeds ÷ Investment)^(1÷Years) − 1  |  MOIC = Exit ÷ Investment
Worked Example — Exit Scenarios
Given: Invest $3M at $10M pre-money  |  7M shares outstanding  |  3-year hold  |  Compare M&A, IPO, Secondary
Step 1 — Establish baseline: price per share at investment:
  PPS = Pre-money ÷ Shares = $10M ÷ 7M = $1.43/share
  Investment buys: $3M ÷ $1.43 = 2.1M shares (30% of company)

Scenario A — M&A at $20M (2× exit):
  Exit per share = $20M ÷ 7M = $2.86/share
  MOIC = $20M ÷ $10M = 2.0×
  IRR = (2.0)^(1÷3) − 1 = 1.26 − 1 = 25.99% ≈ 26%
  Intuition: 2× in 3 years = 26% IRR. Mediocre for VC but possible in a strategic sale.

Scenario B — IPO at $50M (5× exit):
  MOIC = $50M ÷ $10M = 5.0×
  IRR = (5.0)^(1÷3) − 1 = 1.71 − 1 = 70.6%
  Intuition: 5× in 3 years = 71% IRR. This is an excellent outcome for early investors.

Scenario C — Secondary sale at $15M after Year 2:
  Sell 30% of shares at $15M pre-money → $4.5M cash at Year 2
  Remaining 70% stake at hypothetical exit = $10.5M at Year 5
  Total = $4.5M + $10.5M = $15M → MOIC = 1.5× but $4.5M comes 3 years early
  Effective IRR = (4.5/3 + 10.5/10)^(1/5)−1 = similar 1.5× MOIC but reduced risk
Exam tip: IPO typically yields highest IRR because of the large multiple and long hold. But IPO requires 180-day lock-up. Secondary sales provide earlier liquidity (lower risk) but at lower multiples. Compare IRRs to make the right career/finance decision.
Warning: MOIC and IRR tell different stories. A 2× in 1 year (IRR = 100%) beats a 5× in 10 years (IRR = 17%). Always calculate both and compare against the firm's required return hurdle.
15 · VESTING SCHEDULES — The Cliff Explained
Real Talk: Vesting protects both sides. Investors don't want to pay founders who quit early. Founders want job security. The cliff = waiting period before ANY shares vest. Nothing vests until month X, then a chunk vests all at once, then monthly after. Think of it as a staircase: flat → big step → gradual slope.
4-year vesting standard  |  Cliff at 12 or 14 months  |  Monthly or quarterly vest thereafter
Worked Example — Vesting at Month 25 vs Month 6
Given: 4-year vesting (48 months)  |  14-month cliff  |  20% vested at cliff  |  Monthly thereafter  |  Compare: founder leaves at month 25 vs month 6
Step 1 — Map out the vesting schedule:
  Total period = 48 months | Cliff at month 14 | 20% vests at cliff
  Remaining 80% vests over months 15–48 = 34 months
  Monthly vest rate after cliff = 80% ÷ 34 months = 2.353% per month
  Intuition: After the cliff jump, you earn ~2.35% of your shares every month.

Scenario A — Founder leaves at month 25:
  Before cliff (months 1–14): 0% vested — still in cliff period
  At cliff (month 14): 20% vests all at once
  After cliff (months 15–25): 11 months × 2.353% = 25.88%
  Total vested = 20% + 25.88% = 45.88%
  Intuition: At month 25, the founder has climbed 45.88% of the vesting staircase.

Scenario B — Founder leaves at month 6 (before cliff):
  Founder leaves BEFORE the 14-month cliff
  Vested % = 0% — founder gets NOTHING
  Intuition: The cliff exists precisely to prevent this outcome for early quitters.
Intuition tip: Think of vesting like a staircase: up to month 14 you're at the bottom with zero reward. At month 14 you jump up 20% instantly. Then you slowly climb 2.35% per month. Leave at month 25: you've climbed 45.88% of the stairs.
Warning: Monthly vest rate = (100% − cliff%) ÷ (total months − cliff months). Using the wrong denominator is the most common mistake. After cliff: always calculate monthly vest over the REMAINING months only.
16 · WORKING CAPITAL & LIQUIDITY RATIOS
Real Talk: Liquidity ratios answer: "Can we pay our short-term bills?" Current Ratio > 1.5–2 is healthy. Quick Ratio < 1 means even liquidating all receivables won't cover current debts — a warning sign. The Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC) measures how many days cash is tied up in the business cycle.
NWC = CA − CL  |  Current Ratio = CA ÷ CL  |  Quick = (CA − Inventory) ÷ CL  |  CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO
Worked Example — Working Capital & CCC
Given: Cash=$50K  |  A/R=$80K  |  Inventory=$40K  |  A/P=$60K  |  Accrued Exp=$30K  |  Current Tax=$50K  |  Sales=$500K  |  COGS=$300K
Step 1 — Identify Current Assets and Current Liabilities:
  CA = Cash + A/R + Inventory = $50K + $80K + $40K = $170K
  CL = A/P + Accrued Exp + Current Tax = $60K + $30K + $50K = $140K
  NWC = $170K − $140K = $30K (positive = liquidity buffer)
  Intuition: NWC = net short-term liquidity. Positive = more short-term assets than liabilities.

Step 2 — Liquidity Ratios:
  Current Ratio = $170K ÷ $140K = 1.21× (below 1.5 = slightly weak)
  Quick Ratio = ($170K − $40K) ÷ $140K = $130K ÷ $140K = 0.93×
  Intuition: Quick ratio < 1.0 — even selling all receivables won't fully cover current debts. Alert!

Step 3 — Cash Conversion Cycle:
  DSO = (A/R ÷ Sales) × 365 = ($80K ÷ $500K) × 365 = 58.4 days
    Intuition: How many days to collect from customers after a sale?
  DIO = (Inventory ÷ COGS) × 365 = ($40K ÷ $300K) × 365 = 48.7 days
    Intuition: How many days to sell the inventory?
  DPO = (A/P ÷ COGS) × 365 = ($60K ÷ $300K) × 365 = 73.0 days
    Intuition: How many days to pay suppliers?
  CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO = 58.4 + 48.7 − 73.0 = 34.1 days
  Intuition: Cash is tied up 34 days. We collect in 58 days, hold for 49, but delay paying suppliers for 73 days.
Exam tip: Lower CCC is better. Negative CCC means you're collecting from customers BEFORE paying suppliers — a very strong cash position (like Dell historically). Positive CCC means cash is tied up in the business cycle.
Warning: Current Tax is a CURRENT LIABILITY — don't forget to include it in CL. Accrued Expenses are also current liabilities. Make sure to include ALL items that represent short-term obligations.
17 · ARR, CHURN, CAC/LTV, LTV:CAC & EPS
Real Talk: These are the metrics SaaS and subscription businesses live by. ARR = annual run rate of recurring revenue. Churn = revenue lost to cancellations. CAC = cost to acquire a new customer. LTV = lifetime profit per customer. LTV:CAC < 3 = burning money; > 5 = under-investing in growth.
ARR = MRR × 12  |  Net Rev Churn = (MRR Lost − MRR Expansion) ÷ Start MRR  |  CAC = S&M Spend ÷ New Customers
LTV = (ARPU × Gross Margin%) ÷ Churn Rate  |  Diluted EPS = Net Income ÷ (Shares + Net Dilution from Options)
Worked Example — ARR + Churn + CAC/LTV + EPS
Given: Jan MRR=$50K  |  Feb: +$8K new, −$3K churn  |  Sales=$120K/yr  |  Mktg=$80K/yr  |  100 new customers  |  ARPU=$500/mo  |  GM=70%  |  Churn=3%/mo  |  Net Income=$1M  |  Shares=500K  |  Options=100K@$10  |  Price=$25
ARR & Churn:
  Jan ARR = $50K × 12 = $600K annual run rate
  Feb Net New MRR = +$8K − $3K = +$5K MRR
  Feb Gross Churn Rate = $3K ÷ $50K = 6% per month
  Net Rev Churn = ($3K − $0) ÷ $50K = 6% (no expansion data given)

CAC & LTV:
  CAC = (Sales + Mktg) ÷ New Customers = ($120K + $80K) ÷ 100 = $2,000 per customer
  LTV = (ARPU × GM%) ÷ Churn = ($500 × 70%) ÷ 0.03
  = $350 ÷ 0.03 = $11,667 per customer
  LTV:CAC = $11,667 ÷ $2,000 = 5.8× ✓ (above 3 = healthy; above 5 = under-investing slightly)
  Intuition: Each customer generates $11,667 in lifetime gross profit. It cost $2,000 to acquire them.

EPS & Treasury Stock Method:
  Basic EPS = Net Income ÷ Basic Shares = $1M ÷ 500K = $2.00
  Proceeds = 100K options × $10 = $1M (cash to company if exercised)
  Shares repurchased = $1M ÷ $25 = 40K shares (treasury stock method)
  Net dilution = 100K − 40K = 60K shares
  Diluted shares = 500K + 60K = 560K
  Diluted EPS = $1M ÷ 560K = $1.79
Exam tip: LTV:CAC > 3 is generally considered healthy. > 5 means you're not spending enough on growth — consider increasing CAC to acquire more customers faster. Rule of 40: ARR growth% + profit margin% ≥ 40 is healthy SaaS.
Warning: Treasury Stock Method: use the OPTION PRICE (not market price) as proceeds. Proceeds = options × exercise price. Repurchased = proceeds ÷ market price. Net dilution = gross options − repurchased shares.
