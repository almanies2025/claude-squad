1. IRR — Internal Rate of Return

Real Talk: IRR is the discount rate that makes the NPV of a project exactly zero. It answers: "What return does this investment actually generate?" Managers love it because it's a single percentage that captures the whole return profile — but it lies quietly when cash flows aren't "normal" (negative flows only at the start) or when projects are mutually exclusive. The biggest mistake: using IRR to compare projects of different scale or duration — always pair it with NPV.

Key Formulas

0 = ∑(CF_t / (1+IRR)^t)      NPV at IRR = 0 by definition
IRR ≈ r_low + [ NPV_low / (NPV_low - NPV_high) ] × (r_high - r_low)      (interpolation when solving manually)

Worked Example — Simple IRR (single outflow + inflow)

Example: Invest $100 today, receive $130 in Year 3
Given: Initial Investment = $100 (Year 0 outflow)
             Year 3 Cash Inflow = $130
             Project life = 3 years
Step 1: Set up the IRR equation — NPV must equal zero.
     0 = −100 + 130 / (1+IRR)³
Step 2: Rearrange to solve for (1+IRR)³.
     130 / 100 = (1+IRR)³    →     1.30 = (1+IRR)³
Step 3: Take the cube root of both sides.
     (1.30)^(1/3) = 1+IRR    →     1.0914 − 1 = IRR
IRR = 9.14%
Intuition: You put in $100, got back $130 over 3 years — roughly 30% total return, or about 9% per year compounded. If your hurdle rate was 8%, this clears it.

Worked Example — IRR with Interim Cash Flows

Example: Venture capital deal with interim cash flows
Given: Initial Investment = $50,000 (Year 0)
             Year 2 interim cash flow = $10,000
             Year 4 exit cash flow = $80,000
Step 1: Write out the full NPV equation at unknown rate r.
     0 = −50,000 + 10,000/(1+r)² + 80,000/(1+r)^4
Step 2: Try r = 15%. Test NPV at 15%:
     NPV = −50,000 + 10,000/1.3225 + 80,000/1.7490
     NPV = −50,000 + 7,560 + 45,740 = +3,300   (positive → IRR > 15%)
Step 3: Try r = 20%. Test NPV at 20%:
     NPV = −50,000 + 10,000/1.44 + 80,000/2.0736
     NPV = −50,000 + 6,944 + 38,580 = −4,476   (negative → IRR < 20%)
Step 4: Interpolate between 15% and 20%.
     IRR ≈ 15% + [ 3,300 / (3,300 − (−4,476)) ] × (20%−15%)
     IRR ≈ 15% + [ 3,300 / 7,776 ] × 5% ≈ 15% + 2.12% = 17.12%
IRR ≈ 17.1%
The interim $10K at Year 2 reduces risk and boosts IRR vs. a pure Year-4 exit. Never ignore interim cash flows — they matter enormously in VC/startup deals.
Common mistakes: (1) Using IRR on non-normal cash flows (multiple sign changes = multiple IRRs — avoid). (2) Mutually exclusive projects — always use NPV difference, not IRR difference. (3) IRR assumes interim cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself — an unrealistic assumption at high IRRs.

2. DCF — Discounted Cash Flow with Terminal Value

Real Talk: DCF is the foundational valuation method — it values a business today by estimating what all its future cash flows are worth in today's dollars. The twist in growth companies: most of the value often sits in the Terminal Value (TV), not the near-term cash flows. Students get this backward all the time, obsessing over Year 1-5 projections and sloppily estimating TV. Get the TV right and you've done most of the work.

Key Formulas

DCF Total = ∑ [ FCF_t / (1+WACC)^t ]  +  TV / (1+WACC)^n

Gordon Growth Model (TV):   TV = FCF_n × (1+g) / (r − g)
Exit Multiple (TV):              TV = EBITDA_n × Multiple   or   TV = Revenue_n × Multiple

Worked Example — DCF with Terminal Value

Example: 5-year DCF, Gordon Growth Terminal Value
Given: FCF Year 1 = $26,000
             FCF Year 2 = $30,000
             FCF Year 3 = $35,000
             FCF Year 4 = $40,000
             FCF Year 5 = $45,000
             Terminal growth rate (g) = 7%
             Required return (r) = 20%
             WACC = 20%
Step 1: Calculate the Terminal Value at end of Year 5 using Gordon Growth Model.
     TV₅ = FCF₅ × (1+g) / (r − g) = 45,000 × 1.07 / (0.20 − 0.07)
     TV₅ = 48,150 / 0.13 = $370,385
Step 2: Discount each explicit forecast FCF back to Year 0.
     PV₁ = 26,000 / 1.20¹ = 26,000 / 1.2000 = $21,667
     PV₂ = 30,000 / 1.20² = 30,000 / 1.4400 = $20,833
     PV₃ = 35,000 / 1.20³ = 35,000 / 1.7280 = $20,255
     PV₄ = 40,000 / 1.20⁴ = 40,000 / 2.0736 = $19,290
     PV₅ = 45,000 / 1.20⁵ = 45,000 / 2.4883 = $18,084
Step 3: Discount the Terminal Value back 5 years to today.
     PV of TV = 370,385 / 1.20⁵ = 370,385 / 2.4883 = $148,859
Step 4: Sum all discounted cash flows + PV of TV.
     Sum of PV(FCFs) = 21,667 + 20,833 + 20,255 + 19,290 + 18,084 = $100,129
     Total DCF Value = 100,129 + 148,859 = $248,988
Enterprise Value = $248,988   (~$249K)
Notice: TV of ~$370K discounts to ~$149K today — that's 60% of total value. The near-term cash flows are less than half the story. Get g right (must be less than r, always) and use a realistic exit multiple when doing Gordon Growth.
Exam shortcut: When given 4 choice answers for TV, compute TV₅ then discount once: PV(TV) = TV₅ / (1+r)^n. If answers are in the hundreds of thousands and FCFs are small, the TV dominates — so pick the discounted TV closest to the remaining value.

3. WACC — Weighted Average Cost of Capital

Real Talk: WACC is the blended "rent" a company pays to all its capital providers — it's the hurdle rate for any investment decision. The formula weights equity cost (which is riskier and thus costs more) and debt cost (which is cheaper but carries default risk). Students forget that debt is tax-advantaged (interest is deductible) and that the weights should be market-value weights, not book values. Wrong WACC = wrong everything downstream.

Key Formulas

WACC = [ E/(E+D) × r_e ] + [ D/(E+D) × r_d × (1−T) ]

Cost of Equity (CAPM):   r_e = r_f + β × (r_m − r_f)
After-tax cost of debt:   r_d(after-tax) = r_d × (1 − T)

Where:

E= Market value of equity
D= Market value of debt
r_e= Cost of equity (%)
r_d= Pre-tax cost of debt (%)
T= Corporate tax rate
β= Beta (equity risk vs market)
r_f= Risk-free rate
r_m= Market return

CAPM Components:

r_m − r_f= Equity risk premium (ERP)
β × ERP= Beta risk premium
r_f + above= Required equity return

Worked Example — Full WACC Calculation

Example: Company with debt and equity financing
Given: Market cap (E) = $800,000
             Market value of debt (D) = $200,000
             Pre-tax cost of debt (r_d) = 8%
             Corporate tax rate (T) = 40%
             Risk-free rate (r_f) = 4%
             Beta (β) = 1.3
             Market return (r_m) = 14%
Step 1: Calculate the capital structure weights.
     Total Capital = E + D = 800,000 + 200,000 = 1,000,000
     E/(E+D) = 800,000/1,000,000 = 80%
     D/(E+D) = 200,000/1,000,000 = 20%
Step 2: Calculate cost of equity using CAPM.
     r_e = r_f + β(r_m − r_f) = 4% + 1.3×(14%−4%)
     r_e = 4% + 1.3×10% = 4% + 13% = 17%
Step 3: Calculate after-tax cost of debt (interest is deductible).
     r_d(after-tax) = 8% × (1 − 0.40) = 8% × 0.60 = 4.8%
Step 4: Plug into WACC formula — weight each component.
     WACC = (0.80 × 17%) + (0.20 × 4.8%)
     WACC = 13.6% + 0.96% = 14.56%
WACC = 14.56%
Equity costs more than debt because shareholders bear more risk. The 40% tax shield on debt reduces its effective cost from 8% to 4.8%. Notice debt is only 20% of capital, so WACC is dominated by the equity cost. If this company were 60% debt-financed, WACC would be lower (but financial distress risk would rise).
Critical exam traps: (1) Always use market weights, not book values — book equity is historical cost, market cap is current. (2) Use after-tax cost of debt — the tax deduction is real money. (3) If beta > 1, your equity risk premium is above market average. (4) If the company has no debt (or D/E = 0), WACC = r_e.

4. EVA — Economic Value Added (NOPAT Approach)

Real Talk: EVA asks one question: did this business earn more than its cost of capital? Unlike accounting profit (which can be positive while the business destroys value), EVA is true economic profit — operating profit minus the full cost of all capital deployed. It's the clearest signal of whether a company is a value creator or value destroyer. Companies with high ROIC but inadequate returns to their WACC have negative EVA even while appearing profitable on an income statement.

Key Formulas

EVA = NOPAT − (Capital × WACC)

NOPAT = Operating Profit × (1 − Tax Rate)          [= EBIT × (1−T) if no interest]
Capital Charge = Capital × WACC                  [= what all capital providers expect to earn]

Worked Example — EVA (NOPAT Approach)

Example: Manufacturing company evaluating value creation
Given: Operating Profit (EBIT) = $1,200,000
             Capital deployed = $9,000,000
             WACC = 10%
             Corporate tax rate = 40%
Step 1: Calculate NOPAT — operating profit after tax, but BEFORE interest.
     This is the "true" operating cash earnings available to all investors.
     NOPAT = Operating Profit × (1 − Tax Rate)
     NOPAT = 1,200,000 × (1 − 0.40)
     NOPAT = 1,200,000 × 0.60 = $720,000
Step 2: Calculate the Capital Charge — what all capital providers (debt + equity) expect to earn.
     This is the minimum return to keep investors from redeploying capital elsewhere.
     Capital Charge = Capital × WACC
     Capital Charge = 9,000,000 × 0.10 = $900,000
Step 3: Subtract the capital charge from NOPAT.
     EVA = NOPAT − Capital Charge
     EVA = 720,000 − 900,000 = −$180,000
EVA = ($180,000)   Value is being DESTROYED
NOPAT of $720K looks healthy on the surface. But deploying $9M at 10% costs $900K — you're short by $180K. This company is earning below its cost of capital. A positive EVA means the firm is creating wealth; negative EVA means it's destroying it, regardless of what the income statement says.
Students often confuse EBIT-after-tax with NOPAT. Remember: NOPAT assumes no interest expense — it's the operating profit available to both debt AND equity holders before any financing costs. Also watch: if the company has a net loss, NOPAT could still be positive even if GAAP net income is negative — that's the power of the NOPAT framing.

5. Contribution Margin + Sustainable Growth Rate

Real Talk: Contribution Margin tells you how much each sale actually "contributes" to covering fixed costs after variable costs are paid — it's the heartbeat of any variable-cost business. The Sustainable Growth Rate (SGR) then answers: if I reinvest all my profits back into the business at the current return on equity, how fast can I grow without needing external funding? Companies growing faster than their SGR either need to raise capital or will stumble.

Key Formulas

Contribution = Sales − Variable Costs
CM% = Contribution / Sales         (as a percentage of revenue)

Sustainable Growth Rate (SGR) = ROE × Retention Ratio
ROE = Net Income / Shareholders' Equity
Retention Ratio = (Net Income − Dividends) / Net Income = 1 − Payout Ratio

Worked Example — Contribution Margin

Example: Food truck — calculating contribution margin
Given: Total Sales = $100,000
             Variable Costs = $70,000 (food ingredients, direct labor, delivery fees)
             Fixed Costs = $60,000 (truck lease, insurance, base salary)
Step 1: Calculate total contribution — what remains after variable costs to cover fixed costs and profit.
     Contribution = Sales − Variable Costs
     Contribution = 100,000 − 70,000 = $30,000
Step 2: Calculate the contribution margin percentage — contribution per dollar of revenue.
     CM% = Contribution / Sales = 30,000 / 100,000 = 30%
Step 3: Verify fixed cost coverage — each dollar of contribution covers fixed costs.
     Break-even contribution needed = $60,000 fixed costs
     At 30% CM, break-even revenue = 60,000 / 0.30 = $200,000
Contribution = $30,000  |  CM% = 30%
At $100K revenue the food truck is still in loss territory — needs $200K revenue to break even.

Worked Example — Sustainable Growth Rate (SGR)

Example: Startup reinvesting all profits
Given: Net Income = $50,000
             Dividends paid = $0 (all retained)
             Shareholders' Equity = $250,000
Step 1: Calculate ROE — how much return the company generates on equity capital.
     ROE = Net Income / Shareholders' Equity
     ROE = 50,000 / 250,000 = 20%
Step 2: Calculate the Retention Ratio — what percentage of earnings is plowed back in.
     Retention Ratio = (Net Income − Dividends) / Net Income
     Retention Ratio = (50,000 − 0) / 50,000 = 100%
Step 3: Apply the SGR formula — max growth without external funding.
     SGR = ROE × Retention Ratio
     SGR = 20% × 1.0 = 20%
SGR = 20%   (the company can grow up to 20% per year without external capital)
At 20% ROE with 100% retention, the company doubles its equity every ~3.5 years (Rule of 72: 72/20 = 3.6). If it wants to grow faster than 20%, it needs either new equity, more debt, or must accept lower dividend payout. If ROE is below the cost of equity, the SGR is misleading — the company is destroying value even at "sustainable" growth.

6. Terminal Value (Both Approaches) + Comparable Company Valuation

Terminal Value — Gordon Growth Model

Real Talk: Terminal Value is the present value of all cash flows beyond the explicit forecast period — it captures the business's "perpetual" value at the end of your model. The Gordon Growth Model assumes the business grows at a constant rate forever, which is reasonable for mature, stable businesses but a stretch for fast-growing or cyclical ones. The key constraint: g must be less than the required return, or the formula breaks (division by zero or negative).
TV = FCF_n × (1+g) / (r − g)      Gordon Growth Model
TV = EBITDA_n × EV/EBITDA Multiple      Exit Multiple Approach

Worked Example — Terminal Value (Gordon Growth)

Exam Example: Current net income given, FCF growing, Gordon Growth TV
Given: Current Net Income = $20,000
             Next year FCF = $26,000 (Year 1)
             Sustainable growth rate (g) = 7%
             Required return (r) = 20%
Step 1: Identify FCF at the end of the explicit forecast period (Year 1).
     FCF₁ = $26,000   (this grows at 7% perpetually after Year 1)
Step 2: Apply Gordon Growth Model to find TV at end of Year 1.
     TV₁ = FCF₁ × (1+g) / (r − g) = 26,000 × 1.07 / (0.20 − 0.07)
     TV₁ = 27,820 / 0.13 = $214,000
Step 3: Discount TV back to today (PV of TV).
     PV(TV) = TV₁ / (1+r)¹ = 214,000 / 1.20 = $178,333
Step 4: (Optional) Add PV of Year 1 FCF for total enterprise value.
     PV(FCF₁) = 26,000 / 1.20 = $21,667
     Total EV = 21,667 + 178,333 = $200,000
TV (undiscounted) = $214,000  |  PV of TV = $178,333
With Year 1 FCF: Total EV = $200,000
Answer choices: $156,846 | $285,714 | $200,000 | $150,000 | $428,571  →  $200,000 (matches)
Notice the answer $200,000 equals exactly 1/r times next year's FCF when g=0 (26,000/0.20 = 130,000... wait, that's not it). Here the $200K total = Year 1 PV of FCF ($21,667) + discounted TV ($178,333). The TV alone is $214K — so always check which answer the question asks for (TV only vs. total DCF value).

Terminal Value — Exit Multiple Approach

Example: Same company, exit multiple approach
Given: Year 1 EBITDA = $40,000
             Comparable EV/EBITDA multiple observed = 6.0x
             Required return (r) = 20%
Step 1: Apply the exit multiple to Year 1 EBITDA to get TV at Year 1.
     TV₁ = 40,000 × 6.0 = $240,000
Step 2: Discount TV back to today.
     PV(TV) = 240,000 / 1.20 = $200,000
PV of TV (exit multiple) = $200,000   (same as Gordon Growth total — convergence!)
Both approaches can converge on the same answer if your assumptions are internally consistent. The exit multiple approach is often easier in exams because you just multiply — but picking the "right" multiple is the hard part. Gordon Growth is more grounded in fundamentals if you trust your g assumption.

Comparable Company Valuation — P/E Multiples

Real Talk: Comparable company analysis (Comps) values a company by looking at what similar public companies are worth relative to earnings, revenue, or book value. The logic: investors will pay roughly the same multiple for similar earnings quality. The trick: you must use forward-looking EPS (or normalized earnings), not trailing GAAP earnings that include one-time write-offs.

Worked Example — P/E Multiple Approach

Exam Example: P/E multiple from comparable, applied to target EPS
Given: Comparable stock price = $20
             Comparable net income = $20,000
             Comparable shares outstanding = 10,000
             Target EPS = $3.00
Step 1: Calculate the comparable's P/E multiple.
     Comparable EPS = Net Income / Shares = 20,000 / 10,000 = $2.00
     Comparable P/E = Stock Price / EPS = 20 / 2.00 = 10.0x
Step 2: Apply the comparable P/E multiple to the target's EPS.
     Target Stock Price = Target EPS × Comparable P/E
     Target Stock Price = 3.00 × 10.0 = $30.00
Target implied stock price = $30/share
The comparable trades at 10x earnings ($20 price, $2 EPS). If the target earns $3/share with similar business quality and risk, it should also trade at 10x earnings = $30/share. This is the core intuition of relative valuation — similar risk, similar multiples. If the target has higher growth, it deserves a higher P/E; if higher risk, a lower one.
Common mistakes in Comps: (1) Mixing trailing and forward P/E (always use forward/normalized EPS for intrinsic valuation). (2) Ignoring differences in growth rates or risk between comparable and target. (3) Using price/book for financial companies vs. tech companies — different multiples tell different stories. (4) Forgetting to check if the comparable itself is fairly valued before applying its multiple.

Full Comps Table — Multiple Approaches

Given: Comp A price = $20, net income = $20K, shares = 10K, EPS = $2
             Comp B price = $50, net income = $25K, shares = 5K, EPS = $5
             Target net income = $30K, shares = 10K, EPS = $3
Step 1: Calculate each comparable's P/E.
     Comp A P/E = 20 / 2 = 10.0x
     Comp B P/E = 50 / 5 = 10.0x
     Median comp P/E = 10.0x
Step 2: Apply median P/E to target EPS.
     Target Price = 3.00 × 10.0 = $30/share
Target implied share price = $30/share  |  Total equity value = $300,000
