## Executive Summary

This document provides a structured analysis of the legal and regulatory framework governing a Chinese mining company's potential bid for a mining construction contract in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA). The analysis covers Saudi Arabian anti-bribery law, Vision 2030 procurement reforms, China's overseas anti-corruption regulations, the Etimad government procurement platform, the Ministry of Mining, mining sector regulations, and Public Investment Fund (PIF) procurement rules.

**Key Findings:**

- Saudi Arabia has robust anti-bribery legislation with criminal penalties including imprisonment and fines; the law applies to both public officials and private individuals, and explicitly prohibits facilitation payments.
- Saudi Vision 2030 has substantially reformed government procurement toward transparency and competitive tendering; the Etimad platform is central to this reform.
- China has extraterritorial anti-corruption laws that apply to Chinese companies and nationals operating abroad, including potential liability under the Criminal Law Articles 7 (territoriality) and 8 (protective jurisdiction), as well as the 2021 Anti-Foreign Bribery Law.
- The Ministry of Mining (now part of the Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources post-2022 restructuring) administers mining licensing and oversees pre-qualification for mining construction contracts.
- PIF and Saudi Arabian state-owned enterprises (SOEs) follow procurement guidelines that, while more flexible than government procurement, still require transparency and competitive processes for major contracts.

**Complexity Score:** Complex (multiple overlapping jurisdictions, evolving regulatory landscape, significant enforcement risk)

**Critical Recommendation:** Retain qualified legal counsel in both Saudi Arabia and China before proceeding with any bid. The personal connection within the Ministry of Mining must be managed carefully to avoid both legal liability under Saudi anti-bribery law and Chinese overseas anti-corruption regulations.

## 1. Saudi Arabia Anti-Bribery Law

### 1.1 Legal Framework

Saudi Arabia's primary anti-bribery legislation consists of:

**Royal Decree on Combating Bribery (1975) — as amended**  
The cornerstone of Saudi anti-bribery law. Key provisions:

- **Article 1:** Criminalizes the giving, offering, or promising of any money, gift, or benefit to a public official (or to any person for the benefit of a public official) to influence an official act or decision.
- **Article 2:** Criminalizes the receipt by a public official of any money, gift, or benefit in connection with the performance of official duties.
- **Article 3:** Applies to both the briber and the recipient (including intermediaries).
- **Article 4:** Extends liability to corporate entities if bribery is committed by their employees or agents for the company's benefit.

**Criminal Law for the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (Royal Decree No. M/36, 2013)**  
Consolidated criminal code containing provisions on corruption, including:

- **Article 80:** Covers illicit enrichment by public officials.
- **Article 81:** Covers exploitation of official position for personal gain.

**Tenders and Procurement Law (Royal Decree No. M/128, 2019)**  
Replaced the earlier Government Tenders and Procurement Law. Governs government procurement processes and prohibits:

- Conflict of interest in procurement decisions
- Collusion among bidders
- Bribery in connection with public contracts

**Control and Prohibition of Fraud and Bribery Law (2012)**  
Additional layer addressing commercial fraud and bribery in private sector transactions.

### 1.2 What Is Prohibited

Saudi anti-bribery law prohibits:

1. **Active bribery:** Offering, giving, or promising any benefit (money, gift, loan, favor, entertainment, employment, etc.) to a public official to:
   - Influence the performance of an official duty
   - Obtain an unfair advantage in procurement or contracting
   - Facilitate permits, licenses, or approvals

2. **Passive bribery:** A public official receiving, requesting, or agreeing to receive any benefit in connection with official duties

3. **Indirect bribery:** Bribery through intermediaries, agents, or third parties

4. **Kickbacks:** Return of a portion of contract value to officials or their associates

5. **Trading in influence:** Exploiting personal connections with officials to obtain favorable treatment

### 1.3 Penalties

| Offense                            | Imprisonment   | Fine                           | Additional Penalties                                         |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Active bribery of public official  | Up to 10 years | Up to SAR 5 million (~$1.33M)  | Confiscation of bribe proceeds                               |
| Passive bribery by public official | Up to 10 years | Up to SAR 5 million            | Removal from position, lifetime ban from government service  |
| Corporate bribery                  | N/A            | Up to SAR 10 million (~$2.67M) | Debarment from government contracts (temporary or permanent) |
| Trading in influence               | Up to 7 years  | Up to SAR 3 million            | Confiscation of benefits obtained                            |

**Aggravating factors:** Bribery involving government contracts, procurement bids, or mining licenses may result in enhanced penalties. Attempted bribery is also criminalized.

### 1.4 Enforcement

**Enforcement Agencies:**

- **National Anti-Corruption Commission (Nazaha):** Primary enforcement body. Has authority to investigate, arrest, and refer cases for prosecution. Established by Royal Decree in 2011.
- **Board of Grievances (Diwan al-Muzahara):** Administrative court that adjudicates corruption cases involving government officials.
- **Public Prosecution (Al-AMANAH):** Handles criminal prosecutions.
- **Control and Anti-Corruption Authority (Nazaha):** Coordinates with international bodies including the UN Convention Against Corruption (UNCAC).

**Enforcement Trends (2020-2025):**

- Nazaha has significantly increased enforcement activity, with high-profile arrests of senior officials and business executives.
- Saudi Arabia implemented a "zero tolerance" anti-corruption policy under Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman.
- The 2017 anti-corruption campaign ( Ritz-Carlton affair) demonstrated the government's willingness to pursue alleged corruption aggressively.
- Recent enforcement shows active prosecution of both givers and receivers of bribes in government procurement contexts.

### 1.5 Public Officials vs. Private Individuals

**Public officials** are broadly defined under Saudi law to include:

- Employees of government ministries and agencies
- Employees of state-owned enterprises (SOEs)
- Employees of entities performing public functions
- Members of advisory councils
- Anyone performing a public function on behalf of the government

**Key distinction:** While the primary anti-bribery statutes focus on public officials, the Control and Prohibition of Fraud and Bribery Law (2012) and general fraud provisions extend to private-to-private bribery in commercial transactions. However, the most severe penalties apply to public sector corruption.

**For a mining construction bid:** All Ministry of Mining officials, any government employee involved in the tender process, and any SOE employee involved in procurement are public officials under Saudi law. Bribery involving these individuals carries the most severe penalties.

### 1.6 Facilitation Payments

**Saudi Arabia does NOT allow facilitation payments.** Unlike some jurisdictions (most notably the United States under the FCPA's "grease payment" exception), Saudi law does not recognize any exception for small payments to expedite routine government actions.

**This means:**

- Small "facilitation" payments to government officials to expedite permits, licenses, or paperwork are illegal under Saudi law.
- Any such payment, regardless of amount, constitutes bribery.
- This differs from the approach in some other jurisdictions and creates particular risk for companies accustomed to different regulatory environments.

## 2. Saudi Vision 2030 and Procurement Integrity

### 2.1 Vision 2030 Overview

Saudi Vision 2030, launched in 2016, is a comprehensive strategic framework to diversify the Saudi economy away from oil dependence. The Vision includes a strong anti-corruption and good governance pillar.

**Key Governance Principles from Vision 2030:**

- **Transparency:** Government operations, including procurement, should be transparent and subject to public scrutiny.
- **Accountability:** Officials and entities should be accountable for their decisions.
- **Integrity:** Public procurement should be based on merit and free from corruption.
- **Competitiveness:** Government contracts should be awarded through competitive processes.

**National Transformation Program (NTP) Alignment:**  
Vision 2030 is implemented through the NTP, which sets specific targets for governance reform, including procurement digitization and transparency.

### 2.2 Government Tender Process Reforms

The **Public Procurement Law (Royal Decree No. M/128, 2019)** replaced the earlier tender law with significant reforms:

1. **Centralized procurement platform:** The law mandated use of the Etimad platform for all government procurement (discussed in Section 4).

2. **Competitive tendering as default:** All government contracts above certain thresholds must be awarded through open competitive tendering. Exceptions require special approval.

3. **E-tendering requirements:** Government agencies must conduct tenders electronically through Etimad.

4. **Evaluation criteria:** Tenders must be evaluated based on objective criteria (price, quality, experience, compliance with specifications). Criteria must be published in advance.

5. **Bidder complaints mechanism:** The law provides for a formal objection process for aggrieved bidders.

6. **Debarment:** Companies found guilty of corruption, fraud, or collusion may be temporarily or permanently barred from government contracts.

7. **Conflict of interest rules:** Officials involved in procurement must disclose conflicts of interest and recuse themselves from relevant decisions.

8. **Anti-collusion provisions:** Bid-rigging, bid suppression, and other forms of collusion are explicitly prohibited.

### 2.3 Oversight Bodies

**Nazaha (National Anti-Corruption Commission):**

- Receives reports of corruption in government procurement
- Investigates alleged violations
- Refers cases for prosecution
- Operates a public reporting hotline and online portal

**General Court of Audit (GCA):**

- Audits government contracts for compliance with procurement law
- Has authority to identify financial irregularities and refer to relevant authorities

**Zakat, Tax and Customs Authority (ZATCA):**

- For contracts involving foreign companies, reviews transfer pricing and customs documentation

**Authority for the General Organization for Government Estates (GAGE):**

- Oversees real estate and government property transactions

### 2.4 Implications for Mining Construction Bids

For a Chinese mining company considering a mining construction bid:

1. **The tender process is now substantially more transparent than historically** — Vision 2030 reforms have reduced opportunities for informal influence.

2. **Personal connections must be managed carefully** — Any attempt to leverage personal relationships with Ministry officials to gain an unfair advantage could constitute trading in influence or corruption under Saudi law (Section 1).

3. **All procurement interactions should be documented** — Communications with government officials regarding the tender should be formal and through official channels.

4. **The Etimad platform is mandatory** — Registration on and use of Etimad is required for participation in government tenders (Section 4).

## 3. China's Overseas Anti-Corribery Rules Applicable to Chinese Companies Abroad

### 3.1 Applicable Chinese Laws

Chinese companies and nationals operating abroad are subject to several layers of Chinese anti-corruption law with extraterritorial reach:

#### 3.1.1 Criminal Law of the People's Republic of China

**Article 7 (Territorial Jurisdiction — Active Personality Principle):**  
Chinese nationals who commit crimes outside China may be prosecuted under Chinese law if the act constitutes a crime under Chinese law AND is punishable under the law of the country where it occurred, UNLESS the person has already been tried and acquitted or served their punishment in the foreign jurisdiction.

**Article 8 (Protective Jurisdiction):**  
Foreign nationals who commit crimes against Chinese state interests or Chinese nationals outside China may be prosecuted under Chinese law if the act is also considered a crime in the country where it occurred.

**Article 10 (International Judicial Cooperation):**  
Criminal law applies to acts outside China that are covered by international treaties to which China is a party.

**Key implication:** If a Chinese company or its employees engage in bribery in Saudi Arabia, they may be subject to prosecution under Chinese law even if the conduct was local in Saudi Arabia.

#### 3.1.2 Criminal Law Article 391 — Bribery of Foreign Public Officials and International Organization Officials

**Added by the 2011 Amendment (VIII):**  
Chinese law explicitly criminalizes the bribery of foreign public officials and officials of international organizations. This provision has extraterritorial reach.

**Penalty:** If the amount is large, up to 10 years imprisonment; if circumstances are especially serious, 10 years to life imprisonment.

**Important note:** Saudi Arabian government officials are "foreign public officials" under Chinese law. Bribing them constitutes a crime under Chinese Criminal Law.

#### 3.1.3 Anti-Foreign Bribery Law (2021)

Effective April 15, 2021, this law fills gaps in China's previous foreign bribery framework:

**Scope:** Prohibits the giving of property or other benefits to foreign public officials (or officials of international organizations) to obtain improper commercial advantages.

**Key provisions:**

- Covers Chinese companies and their employees, as well as agents, subsidiaries, and intermediaries acting on behalf of Chinese companies abroad
- "Property or other benefits" is interpreted broadly to include money, gifts, entertainment, travel, training, consulting fees, and any other economic advantage
- "Obtaining improper commercial advantages" covers government contracts, licenses, permits, regulatory approvals, etc.
- Both individuals and corporate entities can be held liable
- Voluntary disclosure may result in reduced penalties

**Enforcement:** The Supreme People's Procuratorate has primary jurisdiction and has signaled aggressive enforcement of overseas bribery.

#### 3.1.4 Company Law and State-Owned Enterprise Regulations

**Company Law (2013 Amendment):**  
Establishes corporate liability for bribery by company personnel conducted for the company's benefit. Companies may face fines and debarment from government contracts.

**SOE Regulations:**  
Chinese state-owned enterprises are subject to additional compliance requirements under Party discipline regulations, including:

- Zero tolerance for corruption in overseas operations
- Party Committee (dangwei) oversight of major decisions
- Mandatory compliance reporting

**Important:** If the Chinese mining company is a state-owned enterprise or SOE-affiliated, it faces additional layers of Chinese Communist Party (CCP) disciplinary jurisdiction.

### 3.2 Key Regulations for Overseas Operations

**Guobiao (GB) Standards for Corporate Compliance:**  
China has published voluntary (but widely adopted) compliance management standards for companies operating overseas, including:

- GB/T 35770 — Compliance management systems
- Guidance for Chinese companies on overseas operations under the Belt and Road Initiative

**Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) Regulations:**  
MOFCOM requires Chinese companies investing overseas to:

- Register overseas investments
- Report on compliance with local laws
- Maintain records of anti-corruption compliance

**National Overseas Compliance Program:**  
China has signaled interest in establishing a formal compliance program for overseas operations, similar to the U.S. FCPA clearninghouse concept.

### 3.3 Dual Jurisdiction Risk

A Chinese company bidding in Saudi Arabia faces potential prosecution from BOTH jurisdictions:

| Jurisdiction | Law                                                       | Offense                            | Potential Penalty                                      |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Saudi Arabia | Royal Decree on Bribery (1975)                            | Bribery of public official         | Up to 10 years imprisonment, SAR 5M fine               |
| China        | Criminal Law Article 391; Anti-Foreign Bribery Law (2021) | Bribery of foreign public official | Up to 10 years imprisonment (or life in serious cases) |

**Additionally:** If the company is listed on a U.S. or other international exchange, or uses the U.S. financial system, U.S. FCPA liability may also apply.

### 3.4 Practical Implications

1. **Both Saudi and Chinese law prohibit the same conduct** — There is no "facilitation payment" exception under either system.

2. **The personal connection to the Ministry of Mining is a liability, not an asset** — Attempting to use this connection to gain preferential treatment could constitute bribery or trading in influence under Saudi law AND could trigger Chinese extraterritorial anti-bribery liability.

3. **Due diligence is mandatory** — The company should conduct thorough due diligence on all agents, consultants, and intermediaries it uses in Saudi Arabia to ensure they are not engaged in prohibited payments.

4. **Legal counsel in both jurisdictions is essential** — Retain qualified anti-corruption counsel in both Saudi Arabia and China before proceeding.

5. **Document everything legitimate** — Maintain detailed records of all interactions with Saudi government officials, all due diligence conducted on intermediaries, and all compliance measures.

## 4. Saudi Etimad Procurement Platform

### 4.1 Overview

**Etimad** (meaning "trust" in Arabic) is Saudi Arabia's official government procurement portal, mandated for use by all government agencies and many state-owned enterprises under the Public Procurement Law (2019).

**Managed by:** The Ministry of Finance (MoF) through the Government Procurement Development Center (GPDC).

**URL:** https://etimad.sa

**Purpose:**

- Centralize and digitize government procurement
- Increase transparency
- Enable competition
- Reduce corruption
- Provide audit trails

### 4.2 Registration Requirements for Foreign Companies

#### 4.2.1 Initial Registration

**Required documents typically include:**

1. Commercial registration (from company's home jurisdiction, apostilled or notarized)
2. Certificate of incorporation
3. Tax registration certificate
4. Social insurance certificate (if applicable)
5. Audited financial statements (typically last 2-3 years)
6. ISO quality certifications (e.g., ISO 9001)
7. Experience certificates for similar projects
8. Company profile and organizational chart
9. Authorized representative power of attorney (notarized and apostilled)
10. Saudi Commercial Attaché registration (for foreign companies)

**Cost:** Registration on Etimad is free. However, foreign companies must typically engage a Saudi agent or establish a Saudi subsidiary to bid on most government contracts (see Section 4.2.3).

#### 4.2.2 Supplier Classification

Once registered, companies must obtain a **classification** for the types of work they wish to bid on. Classifications are issued by the **Contractor Classification Committee** under the Ministry of Municipal and Rural Affairs (MOMRA).

**Classification categories:**

- **Building construction**
- **Road and infrastructure**
- **Mining and quarrying**
- **Industrial installations**
- **Electrical works**
- **Mechanical works**
- And other specialized categories

**Classification grades:**

- **First Class:** Large projects (unlimited value)
- **Second Class:** Medium projects (up to SAR 100M)
- **Third Class:** Smaller projects (up to SAR 30M)

**For mining construction specifically:** The company will need to obtain classification in relevant categories, typically "Mining and quarrying" and/or "Industrial installations."

**Requirements for classification:**

- Demonstrated experience in similar projects
- Technical personnel qualifications
- Equipment ownership or access
- Financial capacity (minimum capital requirements vary by class)
- Quality management certifications

**Validity:** Classifications are typically valid for 3-5 years, subject to renewal.

#### 4.2.3 Local Presence Requirements

**Important:** Saudi law generally requires foreign companies to have a **Saudi Arabian legal entity** (limited liability company or joint stock company) registered with the Saudi Arabian General Investment Authority (SAGIA) and the Ministry of Commerce to bid on government contracts.

**Exceptions:**

- Some projects may allow foreign companies to bid directly
- Joint ventures with Saudi partners may satisfy the local presence requirement
- Certain wholly foreign-owned enterprises (as permitted under SAGIA regulations) may bid directly

**Practical implication:** A Chinese company without an existing Saudi entity will likely need to establish a Saudi subsidiary or partner with a Saudi company to participate in a Ministry of Mining tender.

### 4.3 How the Platform Works

#### 4.3.1 Tender Lifecycle on Etimad

1. **Tender Announcement:** Government agency publishes tender details on Etimad, including:
   - Project description and specifications
   - Technical requirements
   - Qualification criteria
   - Evaluation methodology
   - Submission deadline and location
   - Contact information

2. **Pre-qualification (if required):** Some tenders require pre-qualification. Companies submit pre-qualification documents; only pre-qualified companies may submit bids.

3. **Bid Submission:** Companies submit bids electronically through Etimad:
   - Technical proposal
   - Commercial proposal (price)
   - Required certifications and documentation

4. **Bid Opening:** Bids are opened electronically at the specified time. Information about bid amounts may be disclosed publicly for some tender types.

5. **Technical Evaluation:** Technical proposals are evaluated by a committee against pre-published criteria.

6. **Commercial Evaluation:** Commercial proposals of technically compliant bidders are opened and evaluated.

7. **Award:** Contract is awarded to the highest-ranked bidder (or best value bidder depending on tender type).

8. **Contract Execution:** Contract is signed and published on Etimad.

#### 4.3.2 Contract Types

Common contract structures:

- **Unit price contracts:** Price per unit of work performed
- **Lump sum contracts:** Fixed price for entire scope
- **Cost-plus contracts:** Reimbursement of costs plus fixed fee (less common in government contracts)
- **Design-build contracts:** Single contractor for design and construction

### 4.4 Foreign Company Considerations

**Language:** Etimad operates in both Arabic and English. However, Arabic is the controlling language for legal purposes.

**Currency:** Contracts are denominated in Saudi Riyals (SAR). Foreign companies should factor exchange rate risk into pricing.

**Payment terms:** Typically progress payments based on certified work completed.

**Dispute resolution:** Saudi Arabian law governs. Dispute resolution is through Saudi courts or, for some contracts, arbitration (typically under Saudi Arbitration Rules or UNCITRAL).

**Tax considerations:** Foreign companies operating in Saudi Arabia may be subject to:

- Corporate income tax (Zakat for Saudi companies; income tax for foreign companies)
- Withholding tax on payments to foreign entities
- Value-added tax (VAT) at 15% (as of 2020)

## 5. Saudi Ministry of Mining

### 5.1 Ministry Structure and History

**Pre-2022:** Saudi Arabia had a standalone **Ministry of Petroleum and Mineral Resources**.

**2022 Restructuring:** Following a cabinet reshuffle in 2022:

- The Ministry of Petroleum and Mineral Resources was restructured
- The **Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources** was created
- The **Saudi Geological Survey** was transferred to the Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources
- Mining policy and regulation now falls under the Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources

**Current structure relevant to mining:**

- **Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources (MIMR):** Oversees mining sector policy, licensing, and development
- **Saudi Geological Survey (SGS):** Geological surveys, mineral resource assessment
- **Saudi Mining Company (Ma'aden):** State-owned mining company (formerly Saudi Arabian Mining Company), now largely independent but with ongoing government oversight
- **Saudi Authority for Industrial Cities and Technology Zones (MODON):** Industrial zone development and regulation

### 5.2 Role in Awarding Mining Construction Contracts

The Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources is responsible for:

1. **Mining policy and strategy:** Developing and implementing Saudi Arabia's mining strategy, aligned with Vision 2030's goal of developing the mining sector as an economic pillar.

2. **Mineral licensing:** Issuing exploration licenses, exploitation (mining) licenses, and related permits.

3. **Mining lease administration:** Administering mineral agreements and leases.

4. **Regulatory oversight:** Ensuring compliance with mining regulations and environmental standards.

5. **Sector promotion:** Attracting foreign investment into the mining sector.

**For construction contracts specifically:**

- Major mining construction projects (e.g., for Ma'aden or other mining companies) may be tendered through Etimad if government-funded
- For private mining operations, procurement follows the company's internal policies (though Vision 2030 principles apply to SOEs)
- Pre-qualification requirements for mining construction are established by the Ministry in conjunction with MOMRA

### 5.3 Pre-Qualification Requirements

For mining construction projects tendered by or involving the Ministry:

#### 5.3.1 Financial Pre-Qualification

- Minimum registered capital requirements (vary by project size)
- Audited financial statements (typically last 3 years)
- Evidence of working capital adequacy
- Bank reference letter

#### 5.3.2 Technical Pre-Qualification

- Experience on similar mining or heavy industrial construction projects (typically 3-5 projects in the past 5-10 years)
- Technical personnel qualifications (engineers, project managers, specialists)
- Equipment ownership or guaranteed access
- Quality management system (ISO 9001 or equivalent)
- Environmental management system (ISO 14001)
- Occupational health and safety management (ISO 45001 or OSHAS 18001)

#### 5.3.3 Legal Pre-Qualification

- Good standing certificate from home jurisdiction
- No pending legal proceedings related to corruption or fraud
- Compliance with Saudi foreign investment regulations
- Proper business licensing

#### 5.3.4 For Foreign Companies Specifically

- Saudi Arabian legal entity (as discussed in Section 4.2.3)
- Evidence of compliance with home country overseas investment regulations
- Chinese company registration with MOFCOM (if applicable)
- Agent or representative in Saudi Arabia with power of attorney

### 5.4 Current Mining Sector Priorities

Saudi Vision 2030 identifies mining as a key economic diversification sector, particularly:

- **Phosphate:** Ma'aden's Phosphate Company (MPC) is one of the world's largest integrated phosphate producers
- **Gold:** Significant gold mining operations, including Ma'aden's Gold and Copper business
- **Bauxite and Aluminum:** Integration with aluminum smelting
- **Industrial minerals:** Limestone, silica sand, kaolin, and other industrial minerals
- **Rare earth elements:** Emerging focus on critical minerals for energy transition

**Construction opportunities:** New mine development, expansion of existing operations, processing facility construction, and associated infrastructure.

## 6. Saudi Arabia's Mining Sector Regulations

### 6.1 Mining Law Framework

**Primary legislation:**

- **Mining Law (Royal Decree No. M/47, 2014):** The main mining law, which updated earlier regulations and introduced modern mining licensing framework.
- **Implementing Regulations of the Mining Law (2017):** Detailed rules for license applications, terms, and conditions.
- **Investment Law (Royal Decree No. M/1, 2019):** Foreign investment regulations applicable to mining.
- **Environmental regulations:** The Saudi Environmental Law and associated regulations administered by the Saudi Environmental Agency (SEA).

### 6.2 Mining Licenses and Approvals Required for Construction

#### 6.2.1 Exploration License

- **Duration:** Up to 5 years, renewable once for up to 3 years
- **Area:** Up to 100 km²
- **Requirements:** Technical and financial capability, environmental impact assessment (EIA) screening

#### 6.2.2 Exploitation (Mining) License

- **Duration:** Up to 30 years, renewable for similar periods
- **Requirements:**
  - Proven mineral reserves
  - Feasibility study
  - EIA approval
  - Financial guarantee
  - Mining plan

#### 6.2.3 Building/Construction Permits

For construction activities within a mining lease area:

- Construction permit from the relevant municipality
- Building code compliance
- Fire safety approval
- Electrical and mechanical permits for installations

#### 6.2.4 Environmental Approvals

- **Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA):** Required for mining projects. Submitted to the Saudi Environmental Agency (SEA) for review and approval.
- **Environmental permit:** Required before construction begins.
- **Water use permit:** For any water extraction or usage.
- **Waste management plan:** Approval of plan for handling mining waste.

### 6.3 Foreign Ownership Rules

**Post-2019 Investment Law:**  
Saudi Arabia has progressively liberalized foreign investment rules. Under the current Investment Law:

- **Mining sector:** Generally open to 100% foreign ownership.
- **Requirements:** Registration with the Saudi Arabian General Investment Authority (SAGIA) and establishment of a Saudi legal entity.
- **National treatment:** Foreign investors are generally treated the same as Saudi investors once registered, though some sectors retain nationality requirements.

**Joint Ventures:**  
While 100% foreign ownership is permitted, many foreign mining companies operate through joint ventures with Saudi partners, particularly:

- Local knowledge and relationships
- Shared risk
- Compliance with local content requirements (when applicable)

**Local Content Requirements:**  
Saudi Arabia has been developing local content requirements (similar to Saudi Vision 2030's emphasis on Saudization), particularly for:

- Employment (Saudization quotas)
- Procurement from Saudi suppliers
- Technology transfer

**Note:** Local content requirements vary by sector and project. For mining, there is increasing emphasis on developing local supply chains and employment.

### 6.4 Special Economic Zones and Industrial Cities

**Industrial Cities and Technology Zones:**  
The Saudi Authority for Industrial Cities and Technology Zones (MODON) oversees industrial cities where mining processing facilities may be located. Companies operating in these zones receive:

- Streamlined permitting
- Infrastructure support
- Tax incentives (in some cases)

**Rowad 2040:**  
An initiative to develop industrial cities and zones with enhanced incentives for targeted sectors.

## 7. Saudi Public Investment Fund (PIF) and State-Owned Enterprise Procurement

### 7.1 Public Investment Fund Overview

**PIF** is Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund, one of the largest in the world (estimated assets exceeding $700 billion as of 2024). PIF is the investment arm of the Saudi government, established to:

- Diversify the Saudi economy
- Generate long-term returns
- Develop strategic sectors

**Key PIF-owned companies and subsidiaries:**

- Saudi Arabian Oil Company (Aramco) — PIF is the largest shareholder
- Ma'aden (Saudi Arabian Mining Company)
- Saudi Arabian Basic Industries Corporation (SABIC) — PIF acquired majority stake 2020
- Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund investments globally

**For mining specifically:** Ma'aden is the primary Saudi state-owned mining company, operating磷酸, gold, copper, and other mining operations.

### 7.2 PIF Procurement Rules

PIF and PIF-owned companies generally operate under **commercial governance frameworks** rather than strict government procurement rules. However:

#### 7.2.1 PIF Corporate Governance Standards

- PIF has published Corporate Governance Standards for its portfolio companies
- These standards include requirements for:
  - Competitive procurement above certain thresholds
  - Conflict of interest management
  - Board approval for major contracts
  - Transparency and accountability

#### 7.2.2 PIF-Owned Company Procurement

Companies like Ma'aden and SABIC follow their own procurement policies, which:

- Generally require competitive tendering for contracts above defined thresholds
- Include anti-corruption provisions
- Require ethical conduct from suppliers
- Include rights to audit suppliers

**For Ma'aden specifically:**

- Ma'aden has its own procurement portal and supplier registration system
- Supplier qualification requirements similar to government pre-qualification
- The company follows international mining industry procurement standards

### 7.3 State-Owned Enterprise (SOE) Procurement

**Saudi SOEs** include:

- Saudi Aramco (majority PIF-owned)
- SABIC (PIF-owned)
- Ma'aden (PIF-owned)
- Saudi Electricity Company
- Saudi Arabian Airlines
- Others

**SOE Procurement Framework:**

- Each SOE has its own procurement policy, approved by its board
- Government procurement law (2019) applies to some extent to SOEs, particularly for contracts funded by government budgets
- Vision 2030 principles of transparency and competition apply to SOE procurement
- SOEs are subject to Nazaha oversight for corruption prevention

**Key differences from government procurement:**

- More flexibility in procurement methods
- Ability to negotiate directly with suppliers for some contracts
- Faster decision-making in some cases
- Greater emphasis on commercial terms and value optimization

### 7.4 Implications for Chinese Mining Company

If the mining construction bid is for:

1. **A government-funded project (through the Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources):**
   - Full application of the Public Procurement Law (2019)
   - Etimad platform is mandatory
   - Open competitive tendering required

2. **A Ma'aden project (PIF/SOE):**
   - Ma'aden's procurement policies apply
   - Supplier registration with Ma'aden required
   - Competitive tendering generally required above thresholds
   - Anti-corruption provisions in contracts
   - Some flexibility in negotiation for complex projects

3. **A private mining company:**
   - Government procurement law does not directly apply
   - But Vision 2030 principles increasingly influence private sector expectations
   - International anti-corruption standards apply (FCPA if U.S. nexus; UK Bribery Act if UK nexus; Chinese law for Chinese companies)

## 10. Conclusion and Recommendations

### 10.1 Summary of Key Findings

1. **Saudi Arabia has robust anti-bribery laws** with severe criminal penalties that apply to both Saudi and foreign companies and individuals. Facilitation payments are not permitted.

2. **Vision 2030 has transformed Saudi procurement** toward transparency and competition. The Etimad platform is mandatory for government tenders, and the tender process is substantially more transparent than historical norms.

3. **China has extraterritorial anti-corruption laws** that apply to Chinese companies and nationals abroad. Bribing Saudi officials constitutes a crime under Chinese law that can be prosecuted in China.

4. **The personal connection to the Ministry of Mining is a liability**, not an asset, from a legal compliance perspective. Attempting to use it for procurement advantage could result in criminal liability under both Saudi and Chinese law.

5. **Registration and classification on Etimad** is a prerequisite for participating in government tenders. This process takes time and requires local presence.

6. **Foreign companies generally need a Saudi legal entity** to bid on government contracts, either through establishment of a subsidiary or through a joint venture with a Saudi partner.

7. **Mining sector regulations** are well-developed, with clear licensing requirements. The sector is open to 100% foreign ownership.

8. **PIF and SOE procurement** follows commercial governance frameworks that include anti-corruption provisions and competitive tendering requirements.

### 10.2 Core Recommendation

**The company should proceed with extreme caution and only with qualified legal counsel in both jurisdictions.**

The legal and compliance risks are substantial. However, if the company is prepared to:

- Commit to full transparency
- Invest in proper Saudi legal entity establishment
- Develop and implement a robust compliance program
- Engage in the bidding process exclusively through legitimate channels
- Retain qualified legal counsel in both Saudi Arabia and China

...then participation in the tender process may be legally and ethically defensible.
