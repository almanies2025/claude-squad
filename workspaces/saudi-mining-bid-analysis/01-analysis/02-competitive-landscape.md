## Executive Summary

Saudi Arabia's mining sector is undergoing a transformative expansion under Vision 2030, with the Kingdom targeting USD 426 billion in mining GDP contribution by 2035. The sector is shifting from hydrocarbons dependency to a diversified economic pillar, creating substantial infrastructure construction opportunities. This document analyzes the competitive landscape across local Saudi conglomerates, international engineering and construction (E&C) firms, and Chinese state-owned enterprises (SOEs) active in the Kingdom. Key finding: the market is highly competitive at the prime contractor level, with local content requirements and Saudization mandates creating structural barriers for foreign firms. Chinese SOEs possess pricing advantages and state-backed financing, but must navigate heightened scrutiny under both Saudi and Chinese anti-corruption regimes.

## 1. Saudi Mining Sector Growth Under Vision 2030

### Scale of Investment

The Kingdom's mining sector is designated as the "third pillar" of the Saudi economy, alongside oil and petrochemicals. Key targets include:

- **GDP contribution target**: USD 426 billion by 2035 (up from approximately USD 17 billion in 2016)
- **Mining investment target**: USD 170 billion by 2030
- **Non-oil minerals GDP share**: targeted to reach 6-8% by 2030
- **Maaden (Saudi Arabian Mining Company)**: the state-owned flagship, with expansion plans across phosphate, gold, aluminum, and copper

### Targeted Mineral Sectors

| Mineral Sector                      | Key Projects / Initiatives                                                                   | Notable Details                                                                              |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Phosphate**                       | Maaden Waad Al Shamal Phosphate Complex; Phosphate Round B (Al Khabra)                       | Saudi Arabia positioned as global phosphate export hub; fertilizers for domestic agriculture |
| **Gold**                            | Jadid, Manhir, and Birk vessels in the Arabian Shield; Central Arabian Gold Belt exploration | Over 60 known gold occurrences; Maaden Gold and PolyMetal international joint ventures       |
| **Copper**                          | Jabal Sayid copper-zinc mine (operational); Al-Mujan copper project                          | Byproduct of gold operations; growing concentrate export capacity                            |
| **Bauxite / Aluminum**              | Ras Al Khair alumina refinery and aluminum smelter (Maaden)                                  | Integrated aluminum chain from bauxite import to primary aluminum export                     |
| **Limestone / Industrial minerals** | National水泥 programs; cement feedstock for giga-projects                                    | Cement and construction material supply for Vision 2030 giga-projects                        |
| **Rare Earth Elements (REE)**       | Exploration initiatives in the Arabian Shield                                                | Early-stage; attracting international junior miners                                          |
| **Coal**                            | University and industrial cogeneration projects                                              | Limited; focused on import substitution                                                      |

### Infrastructure Being Built

The Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources (MIM) and the Saudi Geological Survey (SGS) have identified infrastructure as a critical bottleneck. Key infrastructure categories:

1. **Haul roads and rail**: 1,500+ km of new mining access roads planned; North-South Railway extension studies
2. **Port facilities**: expanded bulk mineral export terminals at Ras Al Khair, Dammam, and Yanbu
3. **Power infrastructure**: dedicated mining-zone substations; potential 2-3 GW solar buildout for remote mines
4. **Water infrastructure**: desalination supply for mineral processing; groundwater development for remote sites
5. **Processing facilities**: calcination, flotation, leaching, and tailings storage facilities (TSFs)
6. **Giga-project linkages**:mining inputs for NEOM, Red Sea Project, Qiddiya (limestone, quartz, clays)

## 5. Barriers to Entry for Foreign Construction Firms

### 5.1 Saudization Requirements (Nitaqat Program)

The Nitaqat (Saudiization) program imposes minimum Saudi employment ratios on all private sector companies operating in the Kingdom. Failure to comply results in:

- **Green zone**: Compliant; full operational freedom
- **Yellow zone**: Partial restrictions; new visa issuance limited
- **Red zone**: Significant restrictions; expat visa suspension, potential business suspension
- **Non-classified**: Business license suspension

For construction companies, the Saudization threshold varies by contractor classification:

| Classification                  | Minimum Saudi %           | Practical Impact                                 |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| **Premium / Elite contractors** | 10-15% (varies by sector) | Manageable for large international firms         |
| **First-class contractors**     | Higher; ~15-20%           | Challenging; requires active Saudization program |
| **Lower-tier contractors**      | Higher proportionally     | Very challenging; drives up labor costs          |

**Impact on Chinese Bidders**: Chinese firms typically rely on Chinese expatriate labor. Meeting Saudization thresholds requires either (a) employing Saudi nationals in supervisory/administrative roles at above-market wages, or (b) partnering with a local company that provides the Saudi workforce. Both approaches add cost and complexity.

### 5.2 Local Content Requirements

Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 and the Public Investment Fund (PIF) Local Content and Supply Chain Development initiatives impose increasing local content requirements:

- **Maaden and Saudi Aramco** both have mandatory local content targets (typically 40-70% local content by value) for major procurement
- **Construction materials**: Cement, steel reinforcement, aggregates, and precast concrete must often be sourced from Saudi manufacturers
- **Services**: Engineering, project management, and construction services increasingly require Saudi-qualified personnel
- **IFC-linked requirements**: PIF-funded projects may require International Finance Corporation (IFC) standards, which intersect with local content rules

Foreign firms must demonstrate local content plans at pre-qualification stage or bid submission.

### 5.3 Joint Venture Requirements

For many large-scale government and quasi-government projects:

- **Mandatory local partner**: Some project tenders specify that a foreign bidder must have a Saudi-owned partner holding a minimum equity stake (typically 25-51%)
- **Classification requirements**: Foreign firms can pre-qualify directly but must demonstrate Saudi partner capacity for certain contract sizes
- **Risk allocation**: JV structures introduce shared liability and governance complexity; local partner may have limited operational contribution but equal profit share
- **Post-award JV**: Some firms structure JVs only after award, which is viewed unfavorably by Saudi clients

**Practical impact**: Chinese firms seeking large mining construction contracts in Saudi Arabia routinely form JVs with established Saudi partners (e.g., Al Mabani, Nesma, or sector-specific local firms). The quality of the local partner is often a decisive evaluation criterion.

### 5.4 Financial Guarantees and Bonding

| Guarantee Type                  | Typical Requirement              | Notes                                                                   |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Bid bond / Tender guarantee** | 1-3% of estimated contract value | Bank guarantee or insurance bond; valid for bid validity period         |
| **Performance bond**            | 10% of contract value            | Issued by Saudi bank or international bank with Saudi counter-guarantee |
| **Advance payment bond**        | Equal to advance payment amount  | Reduces as advance is recovered through work                            |
| **Retention bond**              | In lieu of 10% retention         | Issued to reduce cash lock-up                                           |
| **Labor and material bond**     | Varies                           | Some owners require this in addition to performance bond                |

Foreign firms without Saudi banking relationships face challenges establishing the requisite guarantees. Establishing a Saudi branch or subsidiary with banking relationships is typically a prerequisite.

### 5.5 Pre-Qualification Requirements

Major projects (particularly Maaden, Aramco, and PIF-linked projects) require pre-qualification before bidding:

- **Financial capacity**: Minimum annual revenue thresholds (e.g., USD 50-500 million for large projects)
- **Technical experience**: Demonstrated experience on similar scope/scale projects (typically 3-5 reference projects)
- **HSSEQ**: Health, Safety, Security, Environment, and Quality management systems
- **In-country presence**: Evidence of office, equipment, or Saudi partner
- **Saudization certificate**: Certificate of compliance from the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development (MHRSD)

Chinese firms new to the Saudi market often struggle to demonstrate Saudi-specific project references, even with strong global credentials.

### 5.6 Intellectual Property and Technology Transfer

Saudi Vision 2030 encourages technology transfer to domestic companies. Foreign firms may be required to:

- **Partner with a Saudi entity** for technology deployment
- **Train Saudi nationals** as part of contract execution
- **Disclose certain proprietary methods** as condition of pre-qualification

For Chinese firms, particularly SOEs, this creates tension with technology protection concerns.

### 5.7 Anti-Corruption Compliance

Both Saudi and Chinese regulatory frameworks impose significant compliance requirements:

**Saudi Framework**:

- **Saudi Anti-Bribery Law** (Royal Decree M/36 of 1995): Criminalizes bribery of public officials; penalties include imprisonment and substantial fines
- **Control and Anti-Corruption Authority (Nazaha)**: Government oversight body investigating public corruption
- **Tender Law** (Royal Decree M/58 of 2007): Governs public procurement; prohibits conflicts of interest and favoritis

**Chinese Framework**:

- **Lianzheng (Anti-Corruption) laws**: Chinese officials and SOE employees are subject to Chinese anti-corruption jurisdiction overseas
- **PRC Criminal Law Article 7 (overseas bribery)**: Chinese citizens and companies can be prosecuted in China for bribing foreign officials
- **China'sBRI Anti-Corruption Initiative**: Xi Jinping-era emphasis on clean Belt and Road Initiative investments

**Implication for the Client**: Given the manager's personal connection within the Saudi Ministry of Mining, any use of that relationship must be carefully structured. Accepting non-public tender information from the ministry contact, or using the relationship to influence evaluation, could constitute a violation of both Saudi and Chinese anti-corruption law.

## 9. Decision Points for Client

1. **What is the specific mining construction scope being targeted?** (Phosphate processing civil works, gold mine infrastructure, access roads, full EPC?) — The competitive set varies significantly by scope.

2. **Is a Saudi joint venture partner already identified?** — This is the single most critical structural requirement. Without a credible local partner, pre-qualification on major Maaden projects will be difficult.

3. **Does the client have any Saudi reference projects?** — Even a small project in Saudi Arabia (even in adjacent sectors like oil/gas infrastructure) significantly strengthens pre-qualification.

4. **What is the client's state financing situation?** — Chinese SOE financing (EXIM Bank, CDB, Silk Road Fund) is a differentiator if available. Commercial-only pricing is less competitive against Korean and local firms.

5. **What is the client's Saudization plan?** — Chinese labor-heavy models face structural challenges. The client must have a credible plan for employing and training Saudi nationals.

6. **How will the Ministry connection be used?** — All interactions with the ministry contact must be carefully documented and limited to publicly available information or legitimate pre-qualification guidance. Any non-public tender information received must be declined.
