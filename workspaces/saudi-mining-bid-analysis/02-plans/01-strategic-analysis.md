## Executive Summary

Saudi Arabia's mining sector is undergoing an unprecedented expansion under Vision 2030, targeting USD 426 billion in mining GDP contribution by 2035. This represents a once-in-a-generation opportunity for a Chinese mining company with existing Saudi market presence. However, the legal and competitive environment demands rigorous compliance discipline. **The personal connection inside the Saudi Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources (MIMR) is a potential liability, not an asset — it must be managed with extreme care to avoid serious criminal exposure under both Saudi and Chinese law.**

The path to winning work runs through transparent, official channels: Etimad registration, a credible Saudi JV partner, and world-class technical credentials. This is also the path that protects everyone involved legally.

## 1. Opportunities

### 1.1 Leveraging the Ministry Connection Ethically and Legally

- **Request publicly available information:** Your contact can legitimately direct you to public resources — Etimad portal registration guides, pre-qualification requirements, published tender schedules, and standard contract terms. This is publicly available information anyone can access; your contact simply saves you search time.
- **Understand evaluation criteria:** The MIMR publishes pre-qualification criteria publicly. Your contact can confirm which criteria are weighted most heavily for specific tenders — this is not confidential.
- **Identify the right person to meet:** Your contact can arrange an introduction to the appropriate technical committee or project manager through official channels, rather than attempting to navigate bureaucracy cold.
- **Clarify regulatory requirements:** Your contact can confirm which classifications, certifications, and registrations are actually required for your specific scope of work — reducing time spent on wrong pursuits.

**Recommended approach:** Frame every interaction around publicly available information and official processes. Never ask for non-public data. Never offer anything of value beyond normal business hospitality (modest meals, standard gifts within gift-holicy limits).

### 1.2 Saudi Arabia's Mining Expansion

- Vision 2030 has designated mining as the "third pillar" of the economy, targeting **USD 426 billion in mining GDP by 2035** and **USD 170 billion in mining investment by 2030**.
- The **Phosphate Round B (Al Khabra)** project is the largest upcoming tender, estimated **USD 5–10 billion**, covering mineral processing infrastructure.
- **Maaden** (Saudi Arabian Mining Company) is the state champion with multiple active expansion projects in gold, phosphate, copper, and aluminum.
- **100% foreign ownership** is permitted under the 2019 Investment Law, removing historical ownership barriers.
- New giga-projects (NEOM, Red Sea Project, Qiddiya) create demand for limestone, quartz, and industrial minerals — feeding construction opportunities.

### 1.3 Etimad Platform — Official Procurement Channel

- All government tenders above threshold values are published on **Etimad** (etimad.sa) — Saudi Arabia's mandatory government procurement platform.
- Foreign companies can register as suppliers, view live tenders, and submit bids electronically.
- Registration requires: commercial registration, ISO certifications (9001, 14001, 45001), audited financial statements, and a nominated Saudi representative or local entity.
- **Action:** Register on Etimad immediately. This is the front door for all legitimate government procurement participation.

### 1.4 Compliance with Local Regulations

- **Ministry of Municipal and Rural Affairs (MOMRA)** classification is required for most construction contracts — your company category and grade must be established.
- **Saudi Arabian General Investment Authority (SAGIA / now Invest Saudi)** oversees foreign investment registration and can facilitate market entry processes.
- **Saudi Standards, Metrology and Quality Organization (SASO)** sets product and construction standards that must be met.

## 2. Risks

### 2.1 Saudi Anti-Bribery Law — Serious Criminal Exposure

Saudi Arabia's anti-bribery framework is robust and actively enforced:

- **Royal Decree on Combating Bribery (1975):** Up to **10 years imprisonment** and up to **SAR 5 million fine** for giving, offering, or promising any benefit to a public official to influence an official act. Applies to both the briber and the recipient.
- **Criminal Law (M/36, 2013):** Articles 80–81 cover illicit enrichment and exploitation of official position by public officials.
- **Tenders and Procurement Law (M/128, 2019):** Prohibits bribery, collusion, and fraud in public procurement.
- **Facilitation payments are explicitly prohibited** — there is no exception for small payments to expedite routine government actions.
- **Trading in influence (mutasallih):** Seeking to use a personal relationship with a government official to gain procurement advantage constitutes a criminal offense under Saudi law, separate from outright bribery.

**Critical point:** Even if your contact acts in good faith, any appearance of using the relationship to gain tender advantage exposes both your company and the contact to serious legal risk.

### 2.2 China's Overseas Anti-Corruption Laws — Dual Liability

Chinese companies and nationals operating abroad face overlapping Chinese jurisdiction:

- **Criminal Law Article 391:** Prohibits bribery of foreign public officials. Violation is a criminal offense in China even if the act occurs abroad.
- **Criminal Law Articles 7 and 8:** Territorial and protective jurisdiction — China can prosecute Chinese nationals for crimes committed overseas that harm China's interests or public order.
- **Anti-Foreign Bribery Law (2021):** Imposes liability on Chinese companies and individuals for bribing foreign officials. Applies extraterritorially. Maximum penalty: **10+ years imprisonment and unlimited fines**.
- **Enforcement trend:** China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Ministry of Commerce have increased enforcement against overseas corruption by Chinese SOEs since 2020, particularly in Belt and Road markets.

**Dual liability risk:** The same act could result in prosecution in both Saudi Arabia and China.

### 2.3 Reputational Risks

- Saudi Vision 2030 has elevated international scrutiny of procurement integrity. Western development finance institutions (DFI) co-financing many Vision 2030 projects apply **World Bank GPN standards** and **OECD anti-bribery conventions**.
- Any hint of procurement corruption would be damaging to: (a) the company's reputation in Saudi Arabia, (b) its relationships with international partners, and (c) its standing with Chinese regulators.
- **Chinese SOEs particularly visible:** China Railway Construction Corporation (CRCC), CSCEC, and other Chinese SOEs are under heightened scrutiny in Middle East markets following documented enforcement actions.

### 2.4 Operational Risks

- **Saudization requirements (Nitaqat):** Companies must meet minimum Saudi employment ratios. Chinese companies with primarily Chinese workforces face significant operational adaptation.
- **Local content requirements:** Major Saudi contracts require 40–70% local content by value. This is a structural challenge for foreign firms without local supply chains.
- **JV equity requirements:** Many government-linked tenders require a Saudi partner holding 25–51% equity. Without an established local partner, your company cannot bid as prime contractor on many opportunities.
- **Financial guarantees:** Letters of guarantee from Saudi banks are required. Without an established Saudi banking relationship, securing tender guarantees is difficult.

## 3. Strategic Recommendations

### 3.1 Immediate Compliance Actions

- **Retain qualified legal counsel in both Saudi Arabia and China** before any further action. This is non-negotiable given the criminal exposure involved.
- **Conduct a formal legal review** of the existing relationship with the MIMR contact. Determine precisely what has been discussed and whether any impropriety has occurred.
- **Establish a compliance protocol** for all interactions with Saudi government officials — including gifts, meals, and travel, consistent with both Saudi and Chinese law.
- **Implement a gift and hospitality register** for all government-facing interactions.

### 3.2 Official Market Entry Steps

1. **Register on Etimad (etimad.sa):** Complete the foreign supplier registration. This is the mandatory first step for all government tender participation.
2. **Obtain MOMRA classification:** Apply for the appropriate contractor grade (Category 1–5, Grade A–D) based on your company's financial and technical credentials.
3. **Register with Invest Saudi (formerly SAGIA):** Confirm your foreign investment registration is current and covers mining services and construction.
4. **Establish or formalize a Saudi legal entity:** Most tenders require a Saudi-registered company. Evaluate whether a 100%-owned subsidiary or a JV structure is appropriate.
5. **Open a Saudi riyal account** at a Saudi bank (Al Rajhi, SNB, Banque Saudi Fransi) to enable financial guarantees and local payments.

### 3.3 JV Partnership Strategy

- **Secure a credible Saudi JV partner before pre-qualification.** This is the single most important structural requirement.
- Ideal JV candidates: Saudi Binladin Group, Al Mabani, Al Rashid/BICC — established civil works firms that lack deep mineral processing expertise and are actively seeking international technical partners.
- Structure the JV to comply with Nitaqat Saudization requirements from day one.
- Ensure the JV agreement clearly establishes prime contractor responsibilities and liability allocation.

### 3.4 Competitive Positioning

- **Lead with technical credentials:** Maaden and Saudi government clients evaluate construction contractors primarily on technical capability, safety record, and delivery track record.
- **Emphasize Chinese financing:** Chinese policy bank financing (EXIM Bank, CDB) tied to Chinese equipment and labor is a significant differentiator in Saudi tenders. Make the financing package a competitive advantage.
- **Build Maaden references:** Consider targeting smaller Maaden maintenance and brownfield contracts first to establish a Saudi track record before competing for greenfield mega-projects.
- **Avoid price-only competition:** Chinese firms competing solely on price have been accused of undercutting local content requirements. Lead on quality, safety, and financing instead.

## 7. EPC Contract Economics and IRR Analysis

_Added 2026-05-10 — based on current market data from Maaden financials, Saudi construction market reports, and competitive intelligence._

### 7.1 The Counterparty — Maaden's Financial Health

**Maaden (Saudi Arabian Mining Company) is in a strong financial position**, which is the most direct indicator of whether your EPC contract will be paid reliably and whether the pipeline of future work is real:

- **2025 full-year revenue:** $10.3 billion — up significantly from $8.7 billion in 2024
- **2025 net profit:** $2 billion — more than doubling year-on-year, a 156% increase driven by higher commodity prices
- **H1 2025 revenue:** $17.93 billion (+23% year-on-year), with EBITDA of $7.25 billion
- **2025 CAPEX guidance:** $7.55–9.55 billion, with ~70% allocated to growth projects
- **EBITDA margin:** approximately 40%+ — the mining operations are highly profitable, supporting EPC contract payments

**Bottom line on counterparty:** Maaden is generating record profits and is in a massive CAPEX cycle. The company has the financial capacity to honor its EPC contract obligations. This is not a distressed counterparty.

### 7.2 Saudi Mining Construction Market Scale

- **Saudi infrastructure construction market:** $68.47 billion in 2026, growing to $87.89 billion by 2031 (5.12% CAGR)
- **Saudi power EPC market alone:** $6.79 billion in 2025, projected $9.85 billion by 2034
- **Chinese EPC firms have already secured over $5 billion in Saudi contracts** in 2022 alone (Source: South China Morning Post via Enterprise Workforce Solutions) — the market is open to Chinese entrants

**2026 market caution flag:** Saudi construction contract awards fell to under **$30 billion in 2025**, approximately **60% below the $71 billion issued in 2024**. This is a significant market contraction. More competition for fewer contracts makes your existing government relationship and current contract position more valuable — and makes any compliance misstep more costly, as losing this position would mean competing in a tighter market.

### 7.3 EPC Contract IRR — Realistic Range

There is no single published IRR benchmark for Saudi mining EPC contracts — this data is proprietary to contractors and investors. However, the IRR can be modeled structurally from its components:

**The IRR Stack for a Chinese EPC Contractor in Saudi Mining**

| Revenue Driver                           | Typical Range                         | IRR Impact                             |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Construction margin (EPC markup)         | 8–15% on contract value               | Primary driver                         |
| Procurement supply chain markup          | 10–25% above equipment cost           | Significant if using Chinese suppliers |
| Saudi "Kingdom premium"                  | 15–30% above global construction cost | Captured if costs controlled           |
| Chinese policy bank financing (EXIM/CDB) | 2–4% vs market 6–8%                   | Reduces financing cost; IRR boost      |
| Delay liquidated damages                 | -0.5–1%/week, capped 5–10%            | IRR destruction risk                   |

**Realistic IRR scenarios:**

| Scenario         | Key Assumptions                                                                        | Estimated IRR |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- |
| **Conservative** | Saudi local bank financing (6–8%), 12-month delay, no procurement markup advantage     | 8–14%         |
| **Base case**    | Chinese policy bank financing (2–4%), on-time delivery, Chinese supply chain markup    | 15–20%        |
| **Optimistic**   | Chinese policy financing + strong procurement margins + Kingdom premium fully captured | 22–28%        |

**The realistic answer is 15–22% IRR** for a well-managed Chinese EPC contractor with Chinese financing and Chinese labor/equipment supply chains executing on time.

### 7.4 The Critical Variable: Financing Structure

The single biggest determinant of your IRR is how the contract is financed:

**Option A — Company balance sheet / self-financed:**

- IRR reflects only the true construction margin
- No cross-border debt exposure
- Appropriate for contract values under $200–300 million
- Cleanest from a compliance perspective

**Option B — Chinese policy bank financing (EXIM Bank, CDB) at 2–4% fixed:**

- Below-market financing is effectively a subsidy — this is the combination that makes Saudi EPC attractive for Chinese firms
- Combined with Chinese labor and equipment at 70–80% of Western-equivalent cost, this is the primary IRR driver
- **Caveat:** Saudi Arabia is not a traditional Belt and Road recipient. Policy bank appetite for Saudi mining projects specifically is not guaranteed. An indicative term sheet should be secured before relying on this structure
- **Risk:** Any political disruption to China-Saudi relations could affect debt servicing and cross-border payment flows

**Option C — Saudi local bank financing at 6–8%:**

- More expensive, but eliminates SAR/RMB currency mismatch on the debt side
- Easier to establish JV banking relationships with Saudi partner
- IRR impact: subtract 3–5 percentage points vs policy bank financing

### 7.5 Currency Risk — The Overlooked IRR Killer

EPC contracts in Saudi Arabia are denominated in **Saudi Riyal (SAR)**. The SAR is pegged to USD at 3.75. However:

- **RMB is floating** against USD — if RMB strengthens 10–15% against the USD over the project life, the SAR-denominated IRR in RMB terms shrinks significantly
- For a RMB-based company, the currency hedge matters enormously
- **Mitigation:** If using Chinese policy bank financing, structure the loan in RMB where possible. If using SAR-denominated contract revenue to service USD-denominated debt, model the currency exposure explicitly before contract signature

### 7.6 Delay Risk — The Most Common IRR Destroyer

Saudi government construction projects routinely run late. The causes are predictable:

- Government approval bottlenecks (permits, inspections, variation order sign-offs)
- Weather — Saudi summers halt outdoor work May–September
- Labor disputes (especially with Saudization quotas)
- Material supply chain disruptions for imported items

**LD exposure model:**

- Typical LD clause: 0.5–1% of contract value per week of delay
- Cap: typically 5–10% of contract value
- For a $500 million contract: 6 months delay at 0.5%/week = ~$13 million in LDs — potentially the entire profit margin

**How successful Chinese contractors manage this:**

- Build acceleration costs into the bid (Chinese labor on 7-day, 12-hour shifts to recover schedule)
- Front-load schedule claims documentation from day one
- Use the Saudi JV partner to manage government relationship and reduce approval delays
